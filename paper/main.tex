\begin{abstract}
Quick Response (QR) codes have become a ubiquitous interface for digital interaction, but their widespread adoption has also enabled a growing class of phishing attacks known as quishing. Existing defenses typically rely either on analyzing decoded payloads, which fails when decoding is not possible, or on visual inspection of QR structures, which often lacks discriminative power. In this work, we introduce Q-Shield, an explainable multimodal framework for quishing detection that models the QR processing pipeline through two complementary stages. The proposed approach combines a pre-decode visual branch, based on a Siamese convolutional network trained with contrastive learning, with a post-decode text branch leveraging a transformer encoder for URL analysis. The outputs of both branches are integrated through a lightweight late-fusion mechanism, enabling robust detection under both decodable and non-decodable conditions.

We evaluate Q-Shield on a heterogeneous benchmark constructed from multiple datasets comprising 21,998 samples with varying QR formats and resolutions. The proposed framework achieves an AUC of 0.9749 and a false-negative rate of 0.057, outperforming visual-only and existing multimodal approaches while meeting common security constraints without threshold calibration. Experimental results demonstrate that multimodal fusion provides complementary gains in accuracy and robustness, particularly under decoding failures and domain shifts. Additionally, Q-Shield incorporates dual-layer explainability using Grad-CAM and SHAP, providing interpretable insights into spatial and feature-level decision processes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quick Response (QR) codes have become a ubiquitous interface for mobile payments, authentication, and information sharing, with adoption accelerating after the COVID-19 pandemic. This rapid integration has also enabled QR code phishing attacks---\textit{quishing}~\cite{sharevski2022phishing}---which exploit the social-engineering dynamics studied by Mitnick~\cite{mitnick2002artdeception} and Hadnagy~\cite{hadnagy2018socialengineering}: QR codes are visually opaque, require a single gesture to activate, and are typically embedded in implicitly trusted contexts (restaurant menus, parking systems, invoices), which attackers exploit to deliver malicious payloads with low user scrutiny. Industry reports identify phishing as a leading initial access vector~\cite{verizon2024dbir} with per-incident costs in the millions~\cite{ibm2024cost}, and recent empirical work shows that quishing is as effective as traditional phishing while being harder to detect for both users and conventional defenses~\cite{weinz2025impact, geisler2024hooked}.

Existing defenses split into two strands. URL-based detectors decode the QR code and classify the resulting payload using lexical or learned features~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet, nejati2026cic}; they achieve high accuracy when the URL is recoverable but become inapplicable when the QR is unreadable (noise, damage, adversarial manipulation) or encodes non-URL data (Wi-Fi credentials, payment instructions)~\cite{trad2025detecting}. Image-based detectors~\cite{trad2025detecting} operate directly on the QR matrix and remain effective without decoding, but their performance lags behind payload-based methods on heterogeneous corpora~\cite{nejati2026cic}. Treating the two as mutually exclusive discards information: visual methods ignore payload-level signal when decoding is possible, and URL-based methods offer no protection when it fails. We therefore frame quishing detection as a \textit{two-stage problem} aligned with the QR processing pipeline (\textit{pre-decode}, where only the image is available, and \textit{post-decode}, where the payload can be analyzed when decoding succeeds), and note that no prior work unifies both signals or systematically evaluates generalization across independently collected corpora~\cite{nejati2026cic, wahid2026alfa}.

We propose \textbf{Q-Shield}, an explainable multimodal framework that unifies both stages. The visual branch employs a Siamese convolutional network trained with contrastive learning to capture structural patterns directly from QR images without decoding. The text branch processes offline-decoded URLs with a transformer encoder---decoding is local-only with no network call or URL execution. A lightweight fusion module integrates both signals along with an explicit undecodability flag, leveraging payload information when available and degrading gracefully to a visual-only detector when decoding fails. Q-Shield is trained and evaluated jointly on heterogeneous corpora to address the cross-dataset gap, and incorporates Grad-CAM and SHAP for dual-layer interpretability of its decisions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Payload- and URL-Based Quishing Detection}

A common line of work treats a QR code as a carrier for a URL and applies phishing-detection techniques after decoding the payload. Amoah and Hayfron-Acquah~\cite{amoah2022qr} used lexical tokenization with classical ML, while Rafsanjani et al.~\cite{rafsanjani2023qsecr} proposed QsecR, a privacy-aware scanner combining blacklist, lexical, host-based, and content-based URL features; recent work confirms that URL lexical features and deep sequence models remain effective for malicious-link detection~\cite{jayaprakash2024heuristic}. These methods exploit the strong semantic signal of decoded URLs but depend on successful decoding and are limited to URL-like payloads---QR codes may also encode Wi-Fi credentials, payment instructions, or arbitrary text, and payload-based detectors cannot operate when the code is damaged, obfuscated, or intentionally crafted to frustrate decoding.

\subsection{Visual and Structural QR Code Analysis}

To avoid dependence on payload extraction, recent work detects malicious QR codes directly from their visual or structural patterns. Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2025detecting} showed that raw QR matrix pixels carry discriminative information, with XGBoost reaching AUC above $0.91$ on a generated benign/phishing corpus, demonstrating the feasibility of pre-decode detection. Akram et al.~\cite{akram2025qr} proposed QR\"iS using interpretable structural features for transparency and deployability, and ALFA~\cite{wahid2026alfa} addressed stylized QR codes that evade standard detectors. Tashtoush et al.~\cite{tashtoush2025comparing} compared URL-based ML with image-based deep learning and reported that visual methods reduce exposure risks at the cost of lower performance. Most of these approaches evaluate on a single dataset or narrow generation pipeline, leaving open whether learned visual patterns generalize across QR versions, resolutions, renderers, and sources.

\subsection{Multimodal and Benchmark-Oriented Approaches}

A recent direction recognizes that image and payload signals are complementary. Khalifa et al.~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet} combined URL and visual representations for phishing detection, while Nejati et al.~\cite{nejati2026cic} released CIC-Trap4Phish, a large multi-format corpus evaluating both CNN image detection and language models over decoded URLs. Existing multimodal studies, however, treat the visual and URL branches as parallel baselines rather than as a single two-stage pipeline, and do not explicitly model the operational distinction between the pre- and post-decode regimes.

\subsection{Human-Centered and Behavioral Studies}

A complementary line of work studies quishing from the user perspective. Sharevski et al.~\cite{sharevski2022phishing} show that users apply less scrutiny to QR codes than to text links; Geisler et al.~\cite{geisler2024hooked} confirm this in field settings, and Weinz et al.~\cite{weinz2025impact} document the organizational impact. Bekavac and Garbin~\cite{bekavac2024qr} study design-oriented countermeasures that make tampering visible at print time. These contributions explain \emph{why} quishing succeeds but do not provide automated detection at scan time, and are therefore complementary to technical detectors such as Q-Shield.

\subsection{Operational QR-Phishing Defenses}

Commercial defenses rely on URL reputation, blocklists, sandboxing, or secure scanner apps; they inherit the same limitations as URL-based research (decoding requirement, reputation coverage, weakness against zero-day or non-URL payloads). Privacy-aware designs such as QsecR~\cite{rafsanjani2023qsecr} mitigate reliance on blacklists but still operate after decoding.

Q-Shield differs by combining both intervention points within a single fused detector: its visual branch operates before decoding (and remains available when the payload cannot be recovered) and its text branch analyzes the locally decoded URL without executing or fetching the destination. A lightweight fusion module integrates both into a single decision, addressing the main gap in recent literature, which tends to choose between visual and payload-based detection rather than treating them as complementary layers of the same defense.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the Q-Shield framework: a pre-decode visual branch and a post-decode text branch operate on the same QR sample $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$, and their logits $\ell_v, \ell_t$ together with an explicit decoding-success flag $f$ are combined through a late-fusion module $g_{\text{fuse}}$:
\begin{equation}
p(y=1\,|\,x) = \sigma\big(g_{\text{fuse}}(\ell_v, \ell_t, f)\big).
\end{equation}
The visual branch operates directly on the rendered QR image (no decoding) and remains the only available signal when the payload cannot be recovered; the text branch analyzes the locally decoded URL when available, ensuring robustness under decoding failures.

\begin{figure*}[htp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{methodology.png}
\caption{Q-Shield pipeline. The QR is preprocessed and routed through two parallel branches: a pre-decode visual branch (Siamese MobileNetV2 $\to$ classifier head) and a post-decode text branch (pyzbar offline decoding $\to$ DistilBERT). Both logits and a decoding-success flag are combined by a late-fusion module; Grad-CAM and SHAP provide explainability over the visual branch.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Visual Branch}

The visual branch is a MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} backbone $h_\psi$ adapted for grayscale input ($3\to 1$ channels), followed by a projection head producing a 128-dimensional L2-normalized embedding. Training proceeds in two phases. \textbf{Phase 1 (contrastive pretraining):} the backbone is trained in a Siamese configuration with margin-based contrastive loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}} = (1-y)\frac{d^2}{2} + y\frac{\max(0, m - d)^2}{2},
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the embedding distance and $m=1.5$, optimized for up to 40 epochs with AdamW, cosine annealing, and early stopping; horizontal flipping is the only augmentation, since rotation would break QR orientation semantics. The contrastive validation loss reaches its minimum at epoch 8 ($0.253$) and pair-classification validation accuracy stabilizes near $71\%$ (training $91.8\%$). \textbf{Phase 2 (supervised fine-tuning):} a four-layer classification head $g_\phi$ maps the embedding to a logit, trained with focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} = -\alpha (1-p_y)^\gamma \log p_y,
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=2$ and $\alpha=0.5$. The backbone is frozen for the first five epochs and then unfrozen for end-to-end optimization; Figure~\ref{fig:phase2} shows the Phase 2 dynamics and justifies the final checkpoint. At test time, horizontal-flip test-time augmentation and a two-seed ensemble further improve robustness without modifying the trained parameters.

\begin{figure}[htp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_phase2_curves.png}
\caption{Phase 2 (focal-loss supervised fine-tuning) dynamics. Purple shading: epochs 1--5 with frozen backbone; green: epochs 6--20 end-to-end. (a) Validation loss reaches its minimum at epoch 8 ($0.053$), after which the train--val gap grows. (b) Validation AUC peaks at $0.8962$ at the same epoch (red marker), corroborating the early-stopping choice.}
\label{fig:phase2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Text Branch}
\label{sec:textbranch}

The text branch decodes the QR matrix locally using pyzbar---producing a UTF-8 string with no network call, DNS resolution, or URL fetch---and feeds the result to a DistilBERT transformer~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} (96-token max length, covering the observed URL distribution) that outputs a classification logit. When decoding fails, samples are mapped to a \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} sentinel token so the model learns decoding failure itself as an informative feature. Training uses focal loss with AdamW and a warmup schedule, converging within a few epochs.

\subsection{Fusion Module}
\label{sec:fusion}

The two branches are combined through late fusion: the input vector $\mathbf{z} = [\ell_v, \ell_t, f]$ is mapped to a final logit by a lightweight MLP and converted to a probability via sigmoid. The fusion module is trained with both branch encoders frozen, reducing the problem to low-dimensional classification. Logit-level fusion is preferred over feature-level fusion because it directly exposes each modality's contribution and keeps the head trivial to train.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate Q-Shield on two complementary corpora (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): the dataset of Trad et al.~\cite{trad2025detecting}, consisting of fixed $69\times69$ binary QR matrices (controlled, homogeneous), and CIC-Trap4Phish~\cite{nejati2026cic}, with over one million real-world QR images at variable resolutions and rendering.

\centering

\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{Trad} & \textbf{CIC-Trap4Phish} \\
\midrule
Total samples & 9,987 & 1,005,738 \\
Format & Binary matrix & Grayscale PNG \\
Resolution & $69\times69$ & 114--582 px \\
QR versions & V13 & V5--V30 \\

For tractability we draw a stratified subset of $50{,}000$ samples per class from CIC; each dataset is then split 80/20 and the validation splits combined into a joint set of $21{,}998$ samples. Training uses the combined training splits to promote cross-domain robustness. The offline pyzbar decoder recovers a URL string from $100\%$ of Trad samples and $95.7\%$ of CIC samples; the remaining $\sim$$4.3\%$ are routed through the \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} sentinel, where the visual branch is the only available signal.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Visual Branch Performance}

The first experiment evaluates the effectiveness of the visual branch in isolation, which represents the system's fallback when QR decoding fails. Results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:visual}.

The strongest configuration, combining a two-seed ensemble with test-time augmentation (TTA), achieves an AUC of 0.9146 and an FNR of 0.199. Compared to handcrafted feature baselines, the deep learning approach improves AUC by approximately 0.10, demonstrating the advantage of learned representations. Furthermore, the ensemble configuration matches the performance reported by Trad et al.~\cite{trad2025detecting} while operating on a dataset an order of magnitude larger and more heterogeneous.

These results establish the visual branch as a strong standalone detector and define the lower bound of system performance in the absence of decoding.

The fusion model resolves this through the explicit undecodability flag $f$, learning when to defer to the visual fallback without manual rule design; while the aggregate AUC gain over text-only is modest ($+0.0157$), it is concentrated in this operationally critical regime.

\subsection{Experiment 6: Explainability}
\label{sec:xai}

The final experiment verifies that the visual branch attends to semantically meaningful regions rather than dataset-specific shortcuts. We combine Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017grad}, computed on the last convolutional layer of the backbone, with SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} attribution on the 128-dimensional embedding to provide complementary spatial and feature-level explanations.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_gradcam_samples.png}
\caption{Grad-CAM overlays for representative benign and phishing samples from both corpora. Attention concentrates near alignment patterns and data-region transitions; aggregated class-difference maps correlate at $r=0.43$ between Trad and CIC, indicating transferable structural cues.}
\label{fig:gradcam}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam} shows that Grad-CAM attention concentrates near alignment patterns and data-region transitions---precisely the substructures that encode the QR payload geometry---and the class-difference attention pattern correlates across corpora (Pearson $r = 0.43$), indicating that the learned cues are transferable rather than dataset artifacts. In the latent space, contrastive pretraining produces an inter- to intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ (benign--phishing distances are approximately $2\times$ larger than intra-class distances), and SHAP analysis identifies a small set of embedding dimensions as the dominant contributors to the classification decision. Together, these analyses provide spatial (where the model looks) and latent (which features drive the decision) interpretability of the visual branch.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Q-Shield introduces a multimodal approach to quishing detection that mirrors the QR processing pipeline by combining a pre-decode visual branch with a post-decode text branch through a lightweight late-fusion module. On a heterogeneous benchmark of $21{,}998$ samples drawn from two independently collected corpora, the fused model reaches AUC $0.9749$ and FNR $0.057$, outperforming prior visual-only and multimodal approaches and meeting the standard $\text{FNR} \leq 0.10$ security tolerance at the default threshold. Cross-dataset evaluation shows that single-corpus training fails to generalize, validating combined training as a methodological requirement, and Grad-CAM with SHAP confirm that the learned representations capture transferable structural cues rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

Limitations include a higher visual-fallback FNR, mild Phase-2 overfitting, reduced performance on high-resolution QR codes, imperfect probability calibration of the visual branch alone, and the absence of contextual information beyond the QR itself. Future work will add a third branch over surrounding message metadata, multi-scale training, adversarial robustness, and localized adaptation for regional payment ecosystems. Q-Shield should be viewed as one layer within a larger security ecosystem, complementary to provenance verification and contextual signals.
