\begin{abstract}
QR code phishing---\textit{quishing}---has become a pressing cybersecurity concern as mobile payment adoption accelerates worldwide. Existing detection methods either decode the payload, exposing the analysis pipeline to the very threat it aims to detect, or rely on handcrafted features that generalize poorly across QR code versions and image formats. We propose Q-Shield, a country-agnostic detection framework that combines Siamese contrastive learning with a lightweight MobileNetV2 backbone to classify QR images directly, without ever decoding their content. The Siamese network learns a 128-dimensional metric space where benign and malicious QR codes are pulled toward distinct regions through contrastive supervision on image pairs. A classification head, trained with focal loss on top of the frozen-then-unfrozen backbone, completes the pipeline. Evaluated on a combined benchmark of 21{,}998 QR samples drawn from the Trad et al.\ corpus and the CIC Trap4Phish 2025 dataset, Q-Shield achieves an AUC of 0.9254 and an F1 score of 0.8580, surpassing the prior state of the art (Trad et al., 0.9133) while validating on an order of magnitude more samples spanning multiple QR versions and image resolutions. Grad-CAM visualization confirms that the model attends to data-region modules rather than finder patterns, while SHAP analysis on embedding dimensions provides quantitative interpretability. At 2.9 million parameters, Q-Shield is compatible with mobile inference. The framework is designed to accommodate future extensions: additional modalities (accompanying text), regional threat contexts (localized datasets), and adversarial robustness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ============================================================

The global adoption of QR codes in payment systems, authentication flows, and public-facing communications has turned an obscure 1990s Japanese invention into a daily security surface. Merchants print QR codes on tables. Governments distribute them in posters. Banks render them inside mobile apps. Attackers noticed. They now distribute QR codes embedded in emails, SMS messages, and physical stickers that, once scanned, redirect users to phishing websites that harvest credentials or install malware. The community has named this attack class \textit{quishing}~\cite{sharevski2022phishing}, and recent empirical studies confirm that it succeeds as frequently as traditional phishing while evading conventional defenses more effectively~\cite{weinz2025impact, geisler2024hooked}.

Quishing succeeds because QR codes occupy a blind spot in conventional phishing defenses. Email and SMS security filters analyze textual content and URL strings; they treat embedded QR images as opaque blobs. The link that a user ultimately follows is never scanned by the security layer. By the time the redirect executes, the damage is already done.

The intuitive countermeasure---scan the QR code, extract the URL, and run existing phishing filters against it---introduces a paradox. Decoding a QR code is the first act of executing its payload. Automated decoding pipelines inherit the very exposure they aim to analyze~\cite{wahsheh2021secure, khalifa2025multiphishnet}. This is particularly dangerous because QR codes can encode data types beyond URLs (Wi-Fi credentials, payment information, scripts), making URL-specific analysis incomplete.

Prior work has begun addressing the first two problems through image-level analysis. Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2025detecting} demonstrated that raw QR pixels carry discriminative signal for malicious intent, achieving AUC $0.913$ on a controlled 9{,}987-sample corpus of fixed-version binary matrices. Wahid et al.~\cite{wahid2025qris, wahid2026alfa} extended structural analysis to payment-specific QR codes with handcrafted features, emphasizing interpretability. Nejati et al.~\cite{nejati2026cic} released the CIC Trap4Phish corpus, pairing QR images with URL metadata across multiple formats and reporting accuracies above $0.95$ on URL-derived features.

\subsection{The cross-dataset generalization gap}

Despite these advances, every prior study we are aware of reports results on a single dataset. None evaluates whether the learned patterns transfer across independently collected QR corpora. Our own cross-dataset experiments (Section~\ref{sec:results}) show that this concern is concrete: a model trained on one public dataset and tested on the other falls to near-chance performance (AUC $0.52$). The visual statistics of fixed-version binary matrices and of variable-resolution antialiased PNGs differ substantially at the pixel level, and mild augmentation cannot bridge that gap. Single-dataset metrics therefore overstate true deployment readiness.

This observation motivates our design. We pose quishing detection as a \textit{domain-invariant learning} problem~\cite{ganin2015dann} and train on both corpora jointly, producing representations that are stable across their differences. The resulting model not only surpasses the prior single-dataset SOTA but does so on an evaluation set that is an order of magnitude larger and more diverse than any previously reported.

\subsection{Contributions}

We propose Q-Shield with five concrete contributions, each closing a documented gap in prior work:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Siamese contrastive architecture for quishing.} We introduce the first application of Siamese networks with contrastive learning to QR phishing detection. Unlike direct-classification baselines that learn class boundaries, our backbone learns a metric space over QR images through paired supervision, producing embeddings with an inter- to intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ (Section~\ref{sec:xai}).

\item \textbf{Cross-dataset generalization as a first-class objective.} We evaluate on a combined 21{,}998-sample benchmark drawn from Trad~\cite{trad2025detecting} and CIC Trap4Phish 2025~\cite{nejati2026cic} simultaneously. This is, to our knowledge, the first published quishing evaluation spanning multiple QR versions \textit{and} multiple image formats within a single test set, and an order of magnitude larger than the largest previously reported evaluation.

\item \textbf{Zero-decoding pipeline.} Q-Shield operates end-to-end on the QR image. The payload is never decoded at inference time, eliminating the exposure paradox of URL-based approaches~\cite{wahsheh2021secure, khalifa2025multiphishnet} and enabling detection on non-URL payloads (Wi-Fi, payment, contact cards) that URL methods cannot handle.

\item \textbf{Calibrated operating points for security deployment.} We report not only the default-threshold AUC ($0.9254$) and F1 ($0.8576$) but also provide a threshold calibration analysis (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}) showing that Q-Shield reaches FNR $\leq 0.10$---the conventional security-tolerance threshold---without retraining. Prior work rarely characterizes this operational trade-off.

\item \textbf{Dual explainability.} We combine Grad-CAM visualization of convolutional attention with SHAP analysis of the learned embedding dimensions, validated \textit{per dataset} to confirm that the learned patterns are not dataset artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:xai}).
\end{itemize}

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews prior work and identifies the gaps Q-Shield closes. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the architecture, the loss functions used in each training phase, and the datasets. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports main results, the ablation study, the cross-dataset benchmark, and threshold calibration. Section~\ref{sec:xai} presents the explainability analysis. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses limitations and future extensions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{URL- and Payload-Based Detection}

The earliest approaches to malicious QR code detection mirror email phishing pipelines: decode the URL, extract lexical and reputation features, classify. Wahsheh and Kadaer~\cite{wahsheh2021secure} proposed a machine learning pipeline operating on URL string features extracted from scanned QR codes. Khalifa et al.~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet} introduced MultiPhishNet, a multimodal deep learning architecture combining URL text with visual representations. Nejati et al.~\cite{nejati2026cic} constructed the CIC Trap4Phish corpus, pairing QR images with URL metadata across multiple document formats, and evaluated classical ML classifiers on URL-derived features.

These methods demonstrate that, given a URL string, classification can achieve high accuracy. Their common limitation is the decoding requirement itself. A QR code must be scanned before URL-level analysis can begin, introducing both the exposure paradox and an incompatibility with non-URL payloads.

\subsection{Pixel-Level Visual Analysis}

Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2025detecting} were, to our knowledge, the first to demonstrate that QR codes contain structural signals sufficient for phishing detection \textit{without decoding}. They trained XGBoost and related classifiers directly on the 4{,}761 raw pixel values of 69$\times$69 QR matrices, achieving AUC 0.9133 on a dataset of 9{,}987 samples. Feature importance analysis revealed that specific pixel positions near alignment patterns carry disproportionate discriminative weight.

Wahid et al.~\cite{wahid2025qris} extended structural analysis to payment-specific QRIS codes with handcrafted features (module density, pattern regularity, error correction level). Their subsequent work~\cite{wahid2026alfa} evaluated robustness of these features under perturbation.

Our work builds on the \textit{no-decoding} insight of Trad and Chehab while departing from their methodology in three ways. First, we replace pixel-level classical ML with a deep CNN backbone (MobileNetV2), obtaining representations invariant to image resolution and QR version. Second, we train the backbone through Siamese contrastive learning rather than direct classification, which decouples feature learning from label memorization. Third, we validate on an order of magnitude more samples spanning multiple dataset origins.

\subsection{Behavioral and Design-Centered Research}

A parallel line of work studies quishing from the user perspective. Sharevski et al.~\cite{sharevski2022phishing} demonstrated experimentally that users exhibit lower vigilance when scanning QR codes relative to clicking text links. Geisler et al.~\cite{geisler2024hooked} confirmed this finding in field conditions. Weinz et al.~\cite{weinz2025impact} documented the organizational impact of quishing on enterprise security. Bekavac and Garbin~\cite{bekavac2024qr} explored design interventions to make tampering visible at print time. These contributions establish why quishing succeeds but do not provide automated detection mechanisms. Our work is complementary: Q-Shield provides the technical detection layer; behavioral interventions address the human factor.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ denote a grayscale QR code image. Our goal is to learn a classifier $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} \to [0, 1]$ that maps $x$ to the probability of being malicious, without decoding $x$ into its underlying payload. The function $f_\theta$ factors into two components:
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(x) = \sigma\big(g_\phi \circ h_\psi(x)\big),
\end{equation}
where $h_\psi: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a convolutional embedding function producing a $d$-dimensional representation, $g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a classification head producing a logit, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid nonlinearity. We train $h_\psi$ and $g_\phi$ in two phases.

\subsection{Siamese Backbone}

The backbone $h_\psi$ is MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} with three modifications. First, the input convolution is reduced from 3 channels to 1 channel to accept grayscale QR images; the pretrained weights are averaged across the RGB dimension to initialize the new layer. Second, the global average pooling output (1280 dimensions) is followed by a projection head with batch normalization, ReLU, dropout (rate 0.35), and a linear projection to $d = 128$. Third, the output is L2-normalized, placing all embeddings on the unit hypersphere. With these modifications, $h_\psi$ has 2.9M parameters.

The Siamese architecture shares $h_\psi$ between two image inputs $(x_1, x_2)$, producing embeddings $(e_1, e_2)$. We train the backbone with contrastive loss~\cite{chopra2005learning}, the canonical choice for Siamese metric learning as surveyed in recent reviews of deep learning objectives~\cite{elharrouss2024lossfunctions}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(e_1, e_2, y) = (1-y)\frac{d^2}{2} + y\frac{\max(0, m - d)^2}{2},
\end{equation}
where $d = \|e_1 - e_2\|_2$, $y = 0$ for same-class pairs and $y = 1$ for different-class pairs, and $m = 1.5$ is the margin.

The loss has an intuitive interpretation. For same-class pairs ($y=0$), the term $d^2/2$ penalizes any residual distance, pulling embeddings of benign--benign or phishing--phishing pairs together. For different-class pairs ($y=1$), the term $\max(0, m-d)^2/2$ activates only if the distance is below the margin, pushing benign--phishing pairs apart until they satisfy $d \geq m$. The margin value $m = 1.5$ was selected by grid search over $\{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5\}$; lower margins induced premature convergence (underfitting at $m=1.0$) and higher margins slowed convergence without measurable gain. We chose contrastive over triplet loss for simplicity of batch construction (pairs are easier to sample uniformly than semi-hard triplets) and because contrastive supervision sufficed to produce an inter- to intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ (Section~\ref{sec:xai}).

\subsection{Phase 1: Contrastive Pretraining}

Training pairs are sampled uniformly: each batch contains an equal split of same-class and different-class pairs, with class (benign or phishing) chosen uniformly at random. We train for up to 40 epochs with 60{,}000 pairs per epoch using AdamW (learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $2 \times 10^{-4}$) and cosine annealing with warm restarts every 15 epochs. Early stopping triggers after 8 epochs without improvement in validation contrastive loss. Horizontal flip augmentation is applied with probability 0.5; rotation is intentionally avoided because QR codes contain semantically meaningful orientation (finder patterns anchor the top-left, top-right, and bottom-left corners).

\subsection{Phase 2: Supervised Classification with Focal Loss}

The classification head $g_\phi$ stacks four linear layers with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout: 128$\to$512$\to$128$\to$32$\to$1. We train with focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal}, which recent surveys~\cite{elharrouss2024lossfunctions} recommend over plain binary cross-entropy in settings where misclassification costs are asymmetric and hard examples dominate the error budget:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}(p, y) = -\alpha_y (1-p_y)^\gamma \log p_y,
\end{equation}
where $p_y$ is the predicted probability of the true class, $\alpha = 0.5$ (classes are approximately balanced after the combined-dataset sampling described below) and $\gamma = 2$. The factor $(1 - p_y)^\gamma$ down-weights easy examples (where $p_y$ is already high) and up-weights hard ones (where $p_y$ is small), focusing gradient signal on the phishing samples that the model currently misclassifies. In security-critical settings this is exactly the desired behavior: a missed phishing QR exposes the user to the attack, whereas a false positive merely triggers a benign warning. Our ablation confirms the practical effect: replacing focal loss with BCE raises false-negative rate from $0.166$ to $0.271$ with only a marginal change in AUC (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

To stabilize training, we freeze the backbone for the first five epochs and train only the head; after epoch 5, the entire network is unfrozen and fine-tuned end-to-end for fifteen additional epochs.

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{Trad et al.~\cite{trad2025detecting}.} 9{,}987 QR codes stored as $69 \times 69$ binary matrices (0 = white module, 1 = black module). All samples correspond to QR Version~13. Classes are balanced (5{,}005 benign; 4{,}982 phishing). We use this dataset to study detection under controlled QR version conditions.

\textbf{CIC Trap4Phish 2025~\cite{nejati2026cic}.} 429{,}976 benign and 575{,}762 malicious QR code images, stored as PNG files of varying dimensions and QR versions. We sample 50{,}000 per class for computational tractability. This dataset exercises the model's ability to generalize across image formats.

We form 80/20 train/validation splits within each dataset and combine them for joint training. Validation uses both datasets simultaneously, yielding 21{,}998 samples.

Three gaps deserve explicit attention. \textbf{Gap 1: cross-dataset evaluation.} Neither Trad nor CIC report performance when the model is trained on one corpus and tested on the other. Our experiments (Table~\ref{tab:cross}) show that under such a zero-shot transfer protocol, models trained on a single dataset degrade to near-random performance (AUC $0.52$) or to classifier collapse (AUC $0.72$ with $\text{FNR} = 0$). Q-Shield integrates Grad-CAM over the convolutional backbone with SHAP over the learned embedding dimensions, and we validate the spatial attention pattern across both datasets separately to rule out dataset-specific artifacts.

Several patterns emerge from Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. First, the Siamese pretraining phase (A2) is the largest single contributor: removing it drops AUC by $0.049$ and raises FNR by $10$ percentage points. Fine-tuning an ImageNet-pretrained MobileNetV2 directly on the binary classification objective, without the prior contrastive stage, fails to extract the same quality of representations that the paired-comparison objective produces. Second, replacing focal loss with standard binary cross-entropy (A3) degrades AUC only marginally ($-0.048$) but increases FNR dramatically ($+0.10$), confirming that focal loss primarily acts on the cost of false negatives rather than overall ranking. The four design choices together account for a roughly $0.05$ AUC improvement, the majority of the gap between our model and the no-pretraining baseline.

We emphasize that these cross-dataset experiments are not a conventional generalization test; rather, they characterize the \textit{domain shift} between the two corpora. CIC images are variable-resolution grayscale PNGs subject to anti-aliasing and rendering artifacts, whereas Trad samples are clean $69 \times 69$ binary matrices. The two distributions differ at the pixel level in ways that exceed what mild augmentation can bridge.

When Q-Shield is trained exclusively on CIC and evaluated on Trad (CV1), the AUC is $0.72$ but the classifier effectively collapses toward the positive class (FNR $\approx 0$, implying precision degrades). The model retains discrimination ability, yet the threshold calibrated on CIC scores does not transfer to Trad's different score distribution. The reverse direction (CV2) is more severe: a backbone optimized on $69 \times 69$ binary matrices has not been exposed to the visual statistics of antialiased PNGs, and performance falls to near chance (AUC $0.52$).

These findings position combined training (CV3) as a form of \textit{domain-invariant learning}~\cite{ganin2015dann}: by including samples from both corpora during both phases of training, the backbone learns a representation robust to both sets of statistics simultaneously. The diversity of the combined corpus is therefore not a convenience but a methodological requirement. The result also has practical implications: metrics reported on a single, visually homogeneous dataset (e.g., a single QR version encoded with a single renderer) should be interpreted cautiously as upper bounds relative to real-world deployment conditions, where QR versions, capture resolutions, and rendering pipelines vary substantially.

Two qualitative patterns emerge. First, the fixed structural patterns of the QR standard---the three finder patterns at the top-left, top-right, and bottom-left corners---receive uniformly low attention across both classes (visible as dark blue regions in the heatmaps). This is the expected behavior: these patterns are class-invariant, so a well-trained classifier should learn to discount them. Second, attention for both classes concentrates on the data-region modules that carry the encoded payload. This confirms that the model's decisions are driven by payload-dependent structure rather than by memorizing positional biases.

Averaging Grad-CAM maps over 200 samples per class (Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam_agg}) reveals a more subtle class-level pattern. This asymmetry is consistent with longer phishing URLs pushing additional data codewords into module positions that benign URLs do not populate. Notably, the finder patterns remain dark in both per-class averages, corroborating that the model does not rely on positional artifacts.

\subsection{SHAP Analysis on Embedding Dimensions}

Treating the 128-dimensional Siamese embedding as a feature vector and the classifier head as the prediction function, we apply kernel SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} to attribute each embedding dimension's contribution to individual predictions. Figure~\ref{fig:shap} ranks the top-20 dimensions by mean absolute SHAP value: the most important dimension contributes $|\text{SHAP}| \approx 0.016$, decreasing smoothly to around $0.005$ at position 20. The remaining 108 dimensions contribute negligibly. This suggests that the embedding is highly over-parameterized for binary classification, and a pruned 32- or 64-dimensional projection head would likely preserve performance while further reducing the memory footprint, which is valuable for mobile deployment.

\subsection{Threshold Calibration for Security Deployment}
\label{sec:calibration}

The metrics in Table~\ref{tab:main} assume the conventional decision threshold of $p = 0.5$. For a security-critical detector, however, a calibrated threshold that privileges recall over precision is often preferable. Because AUC is threshold-independent and already high, the only design choice is where along the ROC curve to operate.

Table~\ref{tab:threshold} reports four operating points on the combined validation set. Sliding the threshold from $0.5$ toward lower values raises recall at the cost of precision, which in our application translates directly to reducing false negatives---the dominant source of harm in phishing detection. Operating at the calibrated threshold that targets FNR $\leq 0.10$ reduces false negatives by over a third while precision decreases by only a few percentage points.

\subsection{Inference Cost}
\label{sec:inference}

Q-Shield contains 2.9M parameters and occupies roughly 12\,MB on disk in FP32. Single-image inference on a mid-range CPU completes in the tens-of-milliseconds range, and on a GPU it completes in under one millisecond with batched throughput in the thousands of images per second. These numbers support the edge-deployability claim: Q-Shield can run as a pre-scan filter inside a mobile QR-reader app without user-perceptible latency.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{What Q-Shield Establishes}

Q-Shield demonstrates that deep metric learning can detect quishing attacks from QR images alone, without payload decoding, across multiple QR versions and image formats, while remaining small enough for mobile deployment. Three claims are supported by the evidence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Structural features alone are sufficient to surpass prior SOTA.} The model never sees URLs, Wi-Fi payloads, or any decoded content, yet achieves AUC 0.9254 on a heterogeneous benchmark, exceeding Trad et al.'s 0.9133.
\item \textit{Learned representations generalize better than handcrafted ones.} Our handcrafted feature baselines plateau near 0.81; the Siamese approach adds 0.11 AUC with no additional feature engineering.
\item \textit{The architecture is interpretable.} Grad-CAM and SHAP together provide pixel-level and feature-level explanations, closing a common gap in prior work.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

Q-Shield has three acknowledged limitations.

\textbf{False negative rate at default threshold.} At the standard decision threshold of $0.5$, Q-Shield's FNR of $0.166$ is above the $\leq 0.10$ target for security-critical deployments. Focal loss reduces FNR substantially relative to BCE (ablation A3: $0.270 \to 0.166$), but does not fully close the gap. Section~\ref{sec:calibration} shows that threshold calibration reduces FNR below $0.10$ at a modest precision cost, making the model deployment-ready when security tolerances are explicit. Cost-sensitive fine-tuning and ensembling remain natural directions for further improvement.

\textbf{Phase-2 overfitting.} During supervised fine-tuning we observed gradual validation-loss divergence after the eighth epoch---training loss continued to decrease while validation loss increased from $0.053$ to $0.103$ by epoch 20. Early stopping (patience $=3$ on val AUC) selects the checkpoint that maximizes generalization, but the phenomenon indicates that our backbone capacity slightly exceeds the information content of the current training corpus. Stronger regularization (MixUp, label smoothing) and additional training data would likely mitigate this further.

\textbf{Unverified URL overlap between datasets.} The Trad dataset does not redistribute URL strings with its pickled QR matrices, preventing direct URL-level overlap checks with CIC. Both datasets draw from different PhishTank crawls and benign corpora (Alexa vs.\ Majestic) recorded at different time windows, which makes substantial overlap unlikely, but this has not been verified empirically. A future release of our pipeline will re-run the full evaluation with a documented overlap check.

\textbf{Unimodal scope.} Q-Shield considers the QR image in isolation, whereas a fielded QR code arrives embedded in a larger context (email subject, SMS body, sender metadata). The architecture leaves room for fusing a textual branch (e.g., a compact language model such as DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert}), but validating this requires paired QR+text datasets that are not publicly available at the scale required.

\textbf{Adversarial robustness.} We have not evaluated Q-Shield against adversarial perturbations of QR modules. Targeted attacks (e.g., patching a small region to flip the prediction) remain an open research question.

\subsection{Future Work}

Three concrete extensions are each worthy of dedicated study:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Multimodal fusion.} Extending Q-Shield with a text branch that processes accompanying context (email, SMS, sender metadata) and fusing both modalities through late concatenation. This is the most direct path to reducing the false negative rate below 10\%.

\item \textbf{Localized deployment.} Q-Shield is country-agnostic by construction, but region-specific phishing campaigns exploit local institutions and linguistic cues. A localized dataset collection and lightweight fine-tuning strategy would enable per-region deployment (e.g., Peruvian financial-services ecosystem, Indian UPI, Brazilian Pix).

\item \textbf{Adversarial robustness.} Empirical evaluation of Q-Shield under module-level adversarial perturbations, with defenses based on adversarial training and randomized smoothing.
\end{enumerate}
