\begin{abstract}
Quick Response (QR) codes have become a ubiquitous interface for digital interaction, but their widespread adoption has also enabled a growing class of phishing attacks known as quishing. Existing defenses typically rely either on analyzing decoded payloads, which fails when decoding is not possible, or on visual inspection of QR structures, which often lacks discriminative power. We introduce Q-Shield, an explainable multimodal framework for quishing detection that models the QR processing pipeline through two complementary stages. The approach combines a pre-decode visual branch, based on a Siamese convolutional network trained with contrastive learning, with a post-decode text branch leveraging a transformer encoder for URL analysis. Both branches are integrated through a lightweight late-fusion mechanism, enabling robust detection under decodable and non-decodable conditions. We evaluate Q-Shield on two complementary datasets with distinct structural and visual properties, capturing both controlled and real-world conditions: the Trad dataset (9,987 samples) and a stratified subset of CIC-Trap4Phish (50,000 samples per class). The visual branch achieves an AUC of 0.9146 and a false-negative rate (FNR) of 0.199, providing a reliable fallback when decoding is unavailable. The full multimodal framework further improves performance, reaching an AUC of 0.9749 and an FNR of 0.057 on a joint evaluation set of 21,998 samples. Cross-dataset evaluation reveals generalization failures under single-corpus training (AUC as low as 0.52), while combined training restores robust performance (AUC 0.8962), establishing heterogeneous training as a methodological requirement for real-world deployment. Q-Shield also incorporates dual-layer explainability using Grad-CAM and SHAP, providing interpretable insights into spatial and feature-level decision processes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quick Response (QR) codes have become a ubiquitous interface for digital interaction, enabling seamless access to mobile payments, authentication, and information sharing. Their adoption accelerated significantly after the COVID-19 pandemic, when contactless interactions became the norm. However, this rapid integration has also introduced new security vulnerabilities, most notably QR code phishing attacks, commonly referred to as \textit{quishing}~\cite{sharevski2022phishing}.

Quishing is fundamentally a social-engineering attack rather than a purely technical exploit: classic treatments of the field~\cite{mitnick2002artdeception, hadnagy2018socialengineering} emphasize that attackers aim to trigger rapid, low-scrutiny user actions. QR codes are particularly effective in this context, since they are visually opaque, require minimal user interaction, and are typically embedded in trusted environments such as restaurant menus, parking systems, or invoices---a combination that fosters implicit trust and that attackers exploit to deliver malicious payloads. Industry reports underscore the severity of this threat: phishing remains a leading initial access vector~\cite{verizon2024dbir}, and the financial impact of phishing-driven breaches reaches several million dollars per incident~\cite{ibm2024cost}. Recent empirical studies further show that quishing can be as effective as traditional phishing while being more difficult for users and conventional defenses to detect~\cite{weinz2025impact, geisler2024hooked}.

Existing defenses fall into two categories. Payload-based detectors decode the QR code and classify the resulting payload---typically a URL---using lexical or learned features~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet, nejati2026cic}; they reach near-perfect accuracy when the payload is recoverable, but are inapplicable when the code is unreadable due to noise, damage, or adversarial manipulation, or when it carries non-URL data such as Wi-Fi credentials or payment instructions~\cite{trad2025detecting}. Image-based detectors instead operate directly on the QR matrix~\cite{trad2025detecting}, remaining available under exactly those conditions, although their performance lags behind payload-based methods on heterogeneous datasets~\cite{nejati2026cic}. Treating the two families as mutually exclusive discards information in both directions: visual methods ignore payload-level evidence when it is available, and URL-based methods offer no protection when it is not.

We therefore frame quishing detection as a \textit{two-stage problem} aligned with the natural QR processing pipeline: a \textit{pre-decode} stage that sees only the visual representation of the code, and a \textit{post-decode} stage that can additionally inspect the payload. Each stage carries complementary evidence, yet prior work neither unifies both signals into a single coherent detection framework nor systematically evaluates generalization across independently collected datasets with varying QR versions and rendering conditions~\cite{nejati2026cic, wahid2026alfa}.

To address these limitations, we propose \textbf{Q-Shield}, an explainable multimodal framework. Its visual branch employs a Siamese convolutional network trained with contrastive learning to capture structural patterns directly from QR images without decoding, while its text branch processes offline-decoded URL strings with a transformer encoder, keeping payload analysis local and adding no exposure risk. A lightweight fusion module integrates both signals together with an explicit undecodability flag, leveraging payload information when available and degrading gracefully to a visual-only detector when decoding fails. Q-Shield is designed with three key objectives: (i) aligning with the operational structure of real-world QR interactions by explicitly modeling pre- and post-decode stages; (ii) prioritizing generalization through training and evaluation across heterogeneous datasets; and (iii) incorporating dual-layer explainability that combines pixel-level attention with latent feature attribution to provide interpretable, verifiable predictions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work on payload-based, visual, multimodal, human-centered, and operational defenses. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the Q-Shield architecture and its three training stages. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the experimental evaluation across six experiments, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes by revisiting the three objectives above.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Payload- and URL-Based Quishing Detection}

A common line of work treats a QR code as a carrier for a URL and applies phishing-detection techniques after decoding. Amoah and Hayfron-Acquah~\cite{amoah2022qr} used lexical tokenization and classical machine learning to distinguish legitimate from phishing URLs embedded in QR codes. Rafsanjani et al.~\cite{rafsanjani2023qsecr} proposed QsecR, a privacy-aware QR scanner combining blacklist, lexical, host-based, and content-based URL features. Recent phishing literature also shows that URL lexical features, host metadata, and deep sequence models remain effective for detecting phishing websites and malicious links~\cite{jayaprakash2024heuristic}. These methods exploit the strong semantic signal of decoded URLs but depend on successful decoding and are limited to URL-like payloads; QR codes may also encode Wi-Fi credentials, payment instructions, or arbitrary text, and payload-based detectors cannot operate when the code is damaged, obfuscated, or intentionally designed to frustrate decoding.

\subsection{Visual and Structural QR Code Analysis}

To avoid dependence on payload extraction, recent studies investigate whether malicious QR codes can be detected directly from their visual or structural patterns. Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2025detecting} showed that raw QR matrix pixels contain discriminative information for quishing detection, with XGBoost achieving AUC above $0.91$ on a generated benign/phishing dataset, demonstrating that pre-decode detection is feasible. Akram et al.~\cite{akram2025qr} proposed QR\"iS, which extracts interpretable structural features for transparency and deployability, and in follow-up work introduced ALFA for fancy or stylized QR codes that can evade standard detectors~\cite{wahid2026alfa}. Tashtoush et al.~\cite{tashtoush2025comparing} compared URL-based ML with image-based deep learning and reported that visual methods reduce preprocessing and exposure risks at the cost of lower performance. Most of these approaches use handcrafted structural features or direct classification and evaluate on a single dataset or narrow generation pipeline, leaving unclear whether learned visual patterns generalize across QR versions, resolutions, renderers, and sources.

\subsection{Multimodal and Benchmark-Oriented Approaches}

A more recent direction recognizes that image and payload signals are complementary. Khalifa et al.~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet} explored multimodal phishing detection by combining URL and visual representations. Nejati et al.~\cite{nejati2026cic} introduced CIC-Trap4Phish, a large multi-format phishing dataset covering Word, Excel, PDF, HTML, and QR-code samples; for QR-based phishing, the benchmark evaluates CNN-based image detection and lightweight language models over decoded URLs, establishing one of the largest recent reference points for quishing research. However, existing multimodal studies generally treat visual and URL branches as parallel baselines or loosely connected components rather than as a single two-stage detection pipeline, and do not explicitly model the operational distinction between the pre-decode and post-decode regimes.

\subsection{Human-Centered and Behavioral Studies}

Quishing is also a social-engineering problem. Sharevski et al.~\cite{sharevski2022phishing} showed that users apply less scrutiny to QR codes than to conventional links; Geisler et al.~\cite{geisler2024hooked} confirmed this in realistic settings; Weinz et al.~\cite{weinz2025impact} analyzed organizational impact; and Bekavac and Garbin~\cite{bekavac2024qr} studied design-oriented countermeasures that make tampering visible to users. These contributions explain \emph{why} quishing succeeds but provide no automated protection at scan time, and are therefore complementary to technical detectors such as Q-Shield.

\subsection{Operational QR-Phishing Defenses}

Commercial defenses rely on URL reputation, blocklists, sandboxing, or secure scanner applications, and inherit the limitations of URL-based research systems: payload extraction, reputation coverage, and weak protection against zero-day or non-URL payloads. Privacy-aware designs such as QsecR~\cite{rafsanjani2023qsecr} reduce reliance on simple blacklists but still operate after decoding. Q-Shield instead combines both intervention points into a single fused detector: the visual branch acts before decoding and remains available when the payload cannot be recovered, the text branch analyzes the locally decoded URL without executing or fetching the destination, and a lightweight fusion module merges both signals into one decision. This addresses the main gap in recent literature, which tends to choose between visual and payload-based detection rather than integrate them as complementary layers of the same defense.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the Q-Shield framework. Given a QR image $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$, the objective is to estimate the probability that the code is malicious; Q-Shield decomposes this into branch-specific predictions combined through a fusion function:
\begin{equation}
p(y=1\,|\,x) = \sigma\big(g_{\text{fuse}}(\ell_v, \ell_t, f)\big),
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\ell_v$ and $\ell_t$ are the logits from the visual and text branches and $f$ indicates whether decoding was successful. The visual branch operates directly on the QR image without decoding and remains the only available signal when the payload cannot be recovered, while the text branch analyzes the decoded payload when available.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{fig_pipeline.png}
\caption{Overview of the Q-Shield framework. A QR input is preprocessed, then routed through two parallel branches: a visual branch that operates directly on the QR image (pre-decode) and a text branch that analyzes the decoded payload when available (post-decode). The resulting logits, together with a decoding-success flag, are combined via a late-fusion module to produce the final phishing probability. Visual explainability (Grad-CAM and SHAP) is integrated within the visual branch.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Visual Branch}

The backbone $h_\psi$ is a MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} adapted for grayscale input by reducing the input channels from 3 to 1, followed by a projection head that maps the global pooling output to a 128-dimensional L2-normalized embedding, enforcing a structured latent space.

\textbf{Phase 1 (Contrastive pretraining).} The backbone is trained in a Siamese configuration~\cite{chopra2005learning} with margin-based contrastive loss
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}} = (1-y)\frac{d^2}{2} + y\frac{\max(0, m - d)^2}{2},
\end{equation}
\noindent where $d$ is the embedding distance and $m=1.5$ is the margin. This objective enforces separation between benign and phishing samples while maintaining intra-class compactness. Training runs for up to 40 epochs using AdamW with cosine annealing and early stopping; horizontal flipping is the only augmentation, since rotation would break QR orientation semantics. Figure~\ref{fig:phase1} reports the resulting dynamics.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_phase1_curves.png}
\caption{Phase 1 (Siamese contrastive pretraining) dynamics over 16 epochs. (a) Contrastive loss decreases steadily, with validation loss minimum at epoch 8 ($0.253$); a cosine warm restart at epoch 15 briefly raises training loss before convergence resumes. (b) Pair-classification accuracy reaches $91.8\%$ on the training set and stabilizes near $71\%$ on the validation set, indicating controlled generalization without severe overfitting.}
\label{fig:phase1}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Phase 2 (Supervised fine-tuning).} A four-layer classification head $g_\phi$ maps the embedding to a logit and is trained with focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} = -\alpha (1-p_y)^\gamma \log p_y,
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=2$ and $\alpha=0.5$. The backbone is frozen for the first 5 epochs to stabilize optimization, then unfrozen for end-to-end fine-tuning. Figure~\ref{fig:phase2} justifies the checkpoint: early stopping at epoch 8, where validation loss is minimal and validation AUC peaks, which also controls the mild train--validation divergence observed afterwards. At test time, horizontal-flip TTA and a two-seed ensemble (\S\ref{sec:results}) further improve robustness without modifying the trained parameters.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_phase2_curves.png}
\caption{Phase 2 (focal-loss supervised fine-tuning) dynamics. Purple shading: epochs 1--5 with frozen backbone; green: epochs 6--20 end-to-end. (a) Validation loss reaches its minimum at epoch 8 ($0.053$), after which the train--val gap grows. (b) Validation AUC peaks at $0.8962$ at the same epoch (red marker), corroborating the early-stopping choice.}
\label{fig:phase2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Text Branch}
\label{sec:textbranch}

The text branch decodes the QR matrix locally using \texttt{pyzbar}, producing a UTF-8 string without any network calls, DNS resolution, or URL fetches. The extracted string is then encoded by a DistilBERT transformer~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} (with a maximum length of 96 tokens, sufficient to cover the observed URL distribution), which outputs a classification logit. When decoding fails, samples are mapped to a \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} sentinel token so the model learns decoding failure itself as an informative feature. The branch is trained with focal loss, AdamW, and a warmup schedule, converging within a few epochs.

\subsection{Fusion Module}
\label{sec:fusion}

The two branches are combined by late fusion over their logits. In~\eqref{eq:fusion}, $g_{\text{fuse}}$ is a two-layer perceptron that maps $\mathbf{z} = [\ell_v, \ell_t, f] \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ through a single hidden layer of 16 ReLU units to a scalar logit, with no normalization---$81$ trainable parameters in total. It is trained on logits cached from the frozen branch encoders, using focal loss with the same setting as Phase 2 ($\gamma=2$, $\alpha=0.5$), AdamW at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 20 epochs; it converges in under a minute on CPU. Because both encoders are frozen, the problem reduces to low-dimensional classification and the head has little capacity to overfit.

The flag $f$ is what makes the two regimes explicit. When decoding succeeds ($f=0$), the text logit dominates, since it is the stronger individual signal; when decoding fails ($f=1$), the sentinel embedding renders $\ell_t$ uninformative and the module learns to weight $\ell_v$ instead, without any hand-written switching rule. Logit-level fusion is preferred over feature-level fusion because it directly exposes each modality's contribution and keeps the head easy to train; we also implemented a feature-level variant concatenating the 128-d visual embedding with the 768-d DistilBERT pooled output, but it requires a full forward pass through both backbones at inference for no measurable gain.

For tractability, we draw a stratified subset of $50{,}000$ samples per class from CIC; each dataset is then split 80/20, and the validation splits are combined into a joint set of $21{,}998$ samples. Training uses the combined training splits to promote cross-domain robustness. The offline \texttt{pyzbar} decoder recovers a string from $100\%$ of Trad samples and $95.7\%$ of CIC samples; the remaining $\sim$$4.3\%$ are routed through the \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} sentinel, where the visual branch becomes the sole discriminative signal. Architectural and optimization settings follow Section~\ref{sec:methodology} and are held fixed across all experiments.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Visual Branch Performance}

The first experiment evaluates the visual branch in isolation, representing the system's fallback when QR decoding fails (Table~\ref{tab:visual}). The strongest configuration (two-seed ensemble + TTA) achieves AUC $0.9146$ and FNR $0.199$, matching the prior visual-only state of the art~\cite{trad2025detecting} on a benchmark eleven times larger and visually heterogeneous, and improving over handcrafted feature baselines by approximately $0.10$ AUC. The single-model configuration achieves an AUC of $0.8962$ at substantially lower cost, making it a deployment-friendly option and a lower bound for system performance when decoding is unavailable.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Multimodal Fusion Performance}

The second experiment compares visual-only, text-only, and fused configurations (Table~\ref{tab:multimodal}). The fused model achieves AUC $0.9749$, F1 $0.936$, and FNR $0.057$, outperforming the visual-only branch by $+7.87$ pp in AUC and achieving a $3.5\times$ reduction in false negatives. The text branch alone is strong but inherently limited by decoding success---on the $\sim$$4.3\%$ undecodable subset, text-only prediction reduces to the constant \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} embedding and the visual branch becomes the sole discriminative signal. The fusion model resolves this by using an explicit undecodability flag, learning when to defer to the visual fallback without manual rules. While the aggregate AUC gain over text-only ($+0.0157$) is modest, it is concentrated in this operationally critical regime; Figure~\ref{fig:cm_roc} confirms balanced classification at the default threshold.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Cross-Dataset Generalization}

The fourth experiment evaluates generalization across datasets to quantify domain-shift effects (Table~\ref{tab:cross}). Models trained on a single corpus degrade sharply on the other: Trad$\to$CIC collapses to near-random (AUC $0.52$, recall $0.51$), and CIC$\to$Trad reaches recall $1.0$ at the cost of predicting nearly every sample as phishing (precision $\sim$$0.50$). Combined training restores AUC to $0.896$, demonstrating that heterogeneous training is necessary for real-world deployment and that single-dataset evaluations overestimate performance.

\subsection{Experiment 5: Operational Trade-offs and Complexity}

Table~\ref{tab:ops} reports threshold calibration for the visual-fallback regime (top) and computational cost per configuration (bottom). In the fallback, $\tau = 0.40$ achieves FNR $\leq 0.10$ at acceptable precision without retraining; the fused model satisfies the same constraint at the default threshold. Threshold selection is therefore a deployment-specific trade-off between missed attacks and false alarms, and $\tau$ should be chosen according to the risk profile of the target environment rather than fixed universally.

On complexity, the visual configuration uses $3.08$\,M parameters ($12$\,MB on disk) and runs at $\sim$$25$\,ms per image on CPU or $6$\,ms on a Tesla T4 ($1{,}156$ images/s at batch 32). The multimodal configuration adds the $66$\,M-parameter DistilBERT encoder, for $69.1$\,M parameters ($267$\,MB) and $\sim$$100$\,ms on CPU; the additional $75$\,ms is the transformer forward pass, which dominates the budget, while the fusion head is negligible at $81$ parameters. Inference is $O(1)$ per sample---independent of corpus size---and both configurations remain within typical mobile interaction budgets. Q-Shield can therefore be configured for different deployment environments, from lightweight visual-only detection to high-accuracy multimodal inference. More broadly, it is best viewed as one layer within a larger security ecosystem, where content-based detection is complemented by provenance verification and contextual signals.

\subsection{Experiment 6: Explainability}
\label{sec:xai}

To verify that the visual branch attends to semantically meaningful regions rather than dataset artifacts, we combine Grad-CAM~\cite{selvaraju2017grad} on the last convolutional layer with SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} on the 128-dimensional embedding. Grad-CAM attention concentrates near alignment patterns and data-region transitions---the substructures that encode the QR payload geometry---and the class-difference attention maps correlate across corpora (Pearson $r = 0.43$), indicating transferable cues rather than dataset-specific shortcuts. In the embedding space, contrastive learning produces an inter-/intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$, and SHAP identifies a small set of latent dimensions as dominant contributors.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The central finding of this work is that multimodal fusion is not an optional refinement for quishing detection: it is the mechanism that turns a detector with FNR $0.202$ into one with FNR $0.057$ while preserving coverage over the QR codes that cannot be decoded at all.

The three objectives set out in Section~I are closed by the evaluation as follows. (i) \emph{Alignment with the operational pipeline}: modeling the pre- and post-decode stages explicitly, and exposing the decoding outcome to the fusion head as a flag, lowers FNR from $0.202$ to $0.057$ on a joint benchmark of $21{,}998$ samples, while the visual branch remains the sole discriminative signal on the $\sim$$4.3\%$ of inputs that never decode. (ii) \emph{Generalization}: cross-dataset evaluation shows that single-corpus training collapses to AUC $0.52$ under domain shift and that combined training restores AUC $0.896$, establishing heterogeneous training as a methodological requirement rather than a tuning choice. (iii) \emph{Dual-layer explainability}: Grad-CAM attention concentrates on alignment patterns and data-region transitions and correlates at $r = 0.43$ across two independently collected corpora, while the contrastively pretrained embedding yields an inter-/intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ with a small set of SHAP-dominant dimensions, supporting the interpretation that the learned cues are structural rather than dataset-specific.

The residual weaknesses are the visual-fallback FNR of $0.199$, mild Phase-2 overfitting controlled by early stopping, degraded performance on high-resolution QR codes, and imperfect probability calibration of the visual branch in isolation; future work will address them through a third branch over surrounding message metadata, multi-scale training, adversarial robustness, and localized adaptation for regional payment ecosystems. Within the scope evaluated here, Q-Shield attains AUC $0.9749$ and FNR $0.057$ at the default threshold, meeting the predefined $\text{FNR} \leq 0.10$ operational target without calibration and improving on the visual-only and handcrafted baselines evaluated in this work on a benchmark an order of magnitude larger than previous single-corpus evaluations.
