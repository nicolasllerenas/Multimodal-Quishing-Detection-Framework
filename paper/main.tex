\begin{abstract}
QR code phishing---commonly referred to as \textit{quishing}---has emerged as a significant cybersecurity threat, particularly in regions where mobile payment platforms drive everyday transactions. Conventional detection methods either require decoding the QR payload, which inadvertently exposes the analysis pipeline to malicious content, or rely on URL-level features that are unavailable when the link is visually encoded. In this paper, we propose a detection framework that operates entirely on the structural and visual properties of QR code images, avoiding payload extraction altogether. We define a set of 25 handcrafted features organized into five groups---geometric, density, complexity, spatial distribution, and run-length texture---and evaluate their discriminative power on two publicly available datasets: the CIC Trap4Phish 2025 corpus (over one million QR images) and the Trad et al.\ dataset (9,987 binary QR matrices). Statistical analysis reveals that horizontal transition frequency (Cohen's $d = -0.76$) and vertical transition frequency ($d = +0.68$) are the strongest individual indicators of malicious intent. A Random Forest classifier trained on these features achieves cross-validated performance consistent with prior benchmarks, and we demonstrate that the learned patterns transfer across datasets. To support interpretability, we incorporate SHAP value analysis, enabling security analysts to trace each prediction back to specific structural anomalies. Our findings confirm that QR code structure alone carries sufficient signal for reliable quishing detection and establish a foundation for lightweight, explainable deployment on resource-constrained mobile devices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ============================================================

The rapid adoption of QR codes as an interface for mobile payments, authentication, and information exchange has reshaped how users interact with digital services. In Latin America alone, platforms such as Yape in Peru (over 15 million active users), Pix in Brazil, and CoDi in Mexico process billions of QR-mediated transactions each year. This ubiquity, however, has attracted adversaries who exploit the opacity of QR codes to distribute phishing links---an attack vector increasingly referred to as \textit{quishing} \cite{sharevski2022phishing}.

Unlike conventional phishing, where malicious URLs appear in plaintext within emails or messages, quishing conceals the payload inside a visual pattern that humans cannot read without machine assistance. This distinction creates three interrelated problems that existing detection systems have struggled to address.

First, traditional email and SMS filters operate on textual content and treat QR code images as opaque attachments, leaving embedded URLs entirely unexamined. We refer to this limitation as \textit{visual blindness}: the security pipeline simply cannot see the threat.

Second, the most intuitive countermeasure---scanning the QR code and analyzing the extracted URL---introduces a paradox. The act of decoding the payload may trigger automatic redirects, expose the scanner to tracking mechanisms, or activate obfuscated redirect chains before any analysis can intervene \cite{wahsheh2021secure}. Moreover, QR codes encode data types beyond URLs (Wi-Fi credentials, payment information, contact cards), rendering URL-specific analysis incomplete.

Third, the social engineering context surrounding quishing attacks varies substantially across regions. Phishing campaigns targeting Peruvian users, for instance, impersonate local banks (BCP, Interbank), fintech platforms (Yape, Plin), and government agencies (SUNAT, Reniec) using culturally specific urgency cues that models trained on English-language corpora fail to recognize \cite{weinz2025impact}.

Recent work by Trad and Chehab \cite{trad2025detecting} demonstrated that QR codes encoding malicious URLs exhibit measurably different pixel-level patterns compared to benign ones, achieving an AUC of 0.9133 with XGBoost on a dataset of 9,987 samples---all without decoding the QR content. This result suggests that the structural complexity imposed by longer, obfuscated URLs leaves a detectable visual fingerprint in the QR module arrangement.

We build on this insight and extend it in three directions. First, we formalize a feature engineering framework comprising 25 structural descriptors that capture geometric, density, complexity, spatial, and textural properties of QR code images. Second, we validate these features on a substantially larger corpus---the CIC Trap4Phish 2025 dataset \cite{nejati2026cic}, which contains over one million QR code images---and demonstrate cross-dataset generalization by training on CIC data and testing on the Trad et al.\ dataset. Third, we integrate SHAP-based explainability to make each classification decision interpretable, addressing a gap identified across nearly all prior approaches \cite{wahid2025qris, khalifa2025multiphishnet}.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item A systematic feature engineering framework for QR code structural analysis, comprising 25 features across five categories, designed to operate without payload decoding.
\item A rigorous statistical characterization of the structural differences between benign and malicious QR codes, including effect size analysis and spatial hotspot identification.
\item Cross-dataset validation demonstrating that structural patterns generalize beyond the training distribution.
\item An explainability layer based on SHAP values that enables security analysts to understand and audit detection decisions.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews prior work on quishing detection and positions our approach relative to existing methods. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes our feature engineering pipeline, datasets, and classification framework. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results, statistical analysis, and explainability findings. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with implications for deployment and directions for future research.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

Research on quishing detection has evolved along several distinct lines, ranging from URL-based analysis that requires payload extraction to behavioral studies focused on user susceptibility. We organize the literature into four categories and identify the gaps that motivate our structural approach.

\subsection{URL-Based Detection Methods}

The earliest attempts to address malicious QR codes adopted the same strategy used for email phishing: decode the embedded URL and analyze its lexical or reputation features. Wahsheh and Kadaer \cite{wahsheh2021secure} proposed a machine learning pipeline that scans QR codes, extracts the URL, and classifies it based on string-level indicators such as domain length, special character frequency, and HTTPS usage. While effective against known phishing patterns, this approach inherits a fundamental limitation: the QR code must be decoded before analysis can begin, exposing the pipeline to the very threat it aims to detect.

More recently, Khalifa et al.\ \cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet} introduced MultiPhishNet, a multimodal deep learning architecture that combines URL features with visual representations. Although the model achieves strong classification performance, its reliance on extracted URLs means it cannot operate in scenarios where decoding is unsafe or where the QR code encodes non-URL data types.

Nejati et al.\ \cite{nejati2026cic} constructed the CIC Trap4Phish dataset, which pairs QR code images with URL metadata across multiple document formats (PDF, HTML, Excel, Word). Their feature extraction pipeline processes over one million samples, but the detection methodology still depends on URL-level attributes derived from decoded payloads. We use their QR image corpus as training data while deliberately avoiding their URL features.

\subsection{Structural and Visual Analysis}

A more recent line of research recognizes that the physical structure of a QR code carries information about its payload without requiring decoding. Trad and Chehab \cite{trad2025detecting} pioneered this direction by training classical machine learning models (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) directly on the 69$\times$69 binary module matrices of QR codes. Their best model achieved an AUC of 0.9133, demonstrating that pixel patterns correlate with phishing risk. Feature importance analysis revealed that specific pixel positions---particularly those near alignment patterns---carry disproportionate discriminative weight.

Wahid et al.\ \cite{wahid2025qris} extended structural analysis to payment-specific QR codes (QRIS), defining features such as module density, error correction level, and pattern regularity. Their work explicitly prioritized interpretability, reporting which structural properties contributed most to classification. In a follow-up study, Wahid et al.\ \cite{wahid2026alfa} evaluated the robustness of structural features under adversarial perturbations, finding that while certain attacks degrade individual features, ensemble models maintain reasonable accuracy.

Our approach builds directly on this structural analysis tradition. We expand the feature set from raw pixel values (Trad et al.) to 25 engineered descriptors that capture higher-order properties---transition frequencies, quadrant asymmetries, run-length statistics---and evaluate them on datasets two orders of magnitude larger than those used in prior structural studies.

\subsection{Behavioral and Organizational Studies}

A complementary body of work examines quishing from the user perspective rather than the detection perspective. Sharevski et al.\ \cite{sharevski2022phishing} conducted controlled experiments revealing that users exhibit lower vigilance when scanning QR codes compared to clicking text links, attributing this gap to the perceived legitimacy of QR interfaces. Geisler et al.\ \cite{geisler2024hooked} confirmed this finding in a field study, observing that contextual cues (physical placement, accompanying text) strongly influence scanning behavior.

Weinz et al.\ \cite{weinz2025impact} investigated the organizational impact of quishing campaigns, documenting how enterprise environments in particular lack policies and technical controls for QR-based threats. Bekavac and Garbin Pranic \cite{bekavac2024qr} explored design interventions that make QR code tampering more visible to end users.

While these studies provide essential context for understanding why quishing succeeds, they do not propose automated detection mechanisms. Our work addresses the technical detection gap while acknowledging that real-world deployment must complement, rather than replace, user awareness initiatives.

\subsection{Comparison and Research Gaps}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes the key characteristics of recent quishing detection approaches. Several patterns emerge from this comparison.

No prior method simultaneously avoids QR decoding, provides feature-level explainability, and validates on large-scale real-world-representative datasets. Structural approaches \cite{trad2025detecting, wahid2025qris} operate safely without decoding but have been evaluated only on small synthetic datasets. URL-based methods \cite{wahsheh2021secure, khalifa2025multiphishnet, nejati2026cic} leverage rich feature spaces but require payload extraction. Behavioral studies \cite{sharevski2022phishing, geisler2024hooked, weinz2025impact} illuminate the human factors but offer no automated countermeasure.

Our work occupies the intersection of structural analysis and explainability, applied at a scale that enables meaningful generalization claims. We retain the safety guarantee of decoding-free analysis while providing SHAP-based transparency that prior structural methods lack.

\subsection{Datasets}

We evaluate our approach on two complementary datasets that differ in scale, format, and provenance.

\textbf{CIC Trap4Phish 2025.} Released by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity at the University of New Brunswick \cite{nejati2026cic}, this corpus contains 429,976 benign and 575,762 malicious QR code images stored as PNG files of varying dimensions. Each image is accompanied by the source URL used to generate the QR code, though we deliberately exclude URL-derived features from our pipeline. The class ratio (57.2\% malicious) introduces a mild imbalance that we address through stratified sampling during evaluation.

\textbf{Trad et al.\ QR Dataset.} This dataset \cite{trad2025detecting} comprises 9,987 QR codes stored as $69 \times 69$ binary matrices (0 = white module, 1 = black module), with 5,005 benign and 4,982 phishing samples. All QR codes correspond to Version~13 (69 modules per side), which controls for version as a confounding variable but limits ecological validity. We use this dataset primarily for cross-dataset validation: models trained on CIC features are tested against Trad samples to assess generalization.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

We define 25 structural features organized into five groups. Each feature is computed from the binarized QR image without accessing the encoded payload.

\textbf{Group A: Geometric properties} (4 features). Image width, height, total pixel count, and aspect ratio. For the CIC dataset, these vary across samples and correlate with QR version, which in turn reflects payload complexity.

\textbf{Group B: Module density} (8 features). The black pixel ratio measures the fraction of data-carrying modules. We subdivide the image into four quadrants---top-left, top-right, bottom-left (containing fixed finder patterns), and bottom-right (dominated by data modules)---and compute per-quadrant density, inter-quadrant standard deviation, range, and the difference between average finder-corner density and data-corner density. The rationale is that longer payloads force additional data modules into the bottom-right region, creating measurable asymmetry.

\textbf{Group C: Complexity metrics} (5 features). Horizontal and vertical transition counts (the number of black-to-white and white-to-black changes per row or column, respectively) serve as proxies for encoding density. Shannon entropy quantifies the information content of the pixel value distribution. Edge density, computed as the mean absolute gradient, captures visual complexity at a finer granularity than transition counts alone.

\textbf{Group D: Spatial distribution} (5 features). Row-wise and column-wise density standard deviations measure the uniformity of data distribution. We further partition the image into a center region (excluding the outer $\lfloor h/6 \rfloor$ rows and columns) and a border region, computing density for each and their difference. This captures how payload data concentrates relative to the fixed timing and alignment patterns at the QR periphery.

\textbf{Group E: Run-length texture} (3 features). For each row, we compute the lengths of consecutive same-color pixel runs and aggregate their mean and standard deviation across all rows. These statistics approximate the QR module size and its regularity, which differ between simple (short URL) and complex (long, obfuscated URL) payloads.

\subsection{Classification Models}

We train three ensemble classifiers that have demonstrated strong performance on tabular feature data in prior phishing detection studies \cite{trad2025detecting, nejati2026cic}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random Forest (RF)}: 200 trees, maximum depth of 15, with Gini impurity splitting. RF provides inherent feature importance estimates and is robust to feature scale differences.
\item \textbf{XGBoost}: Gradient-boosted trees with a learning rate of 0.1, maximum depth of 6, and 200 estimators. We include L2 regularization ($\lambda = 1.0$) to mitigate overfitting on the larger CIC dataset.
\item \textbf{LightGBM}: Leaf-wise gradient boosting with 200 iterations and a maximum of 31 leaves. LightGBM is included for its efficiency on large-scale data, which is relevant for the CIC corpus.
\end{itemize}

All features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance prior to training. Evaluation uses stratified 5-fold cross-validation, with AUC-ROC as the primary metric. We additionally report precision, recall, F1-score, and the false negative rate, the latter being of particular importance in security contexts where missed attacks carry asymmetric cost.

\subsection{Explainability via SHAP}

To provide post-hoc interpretability, we apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) \cite{lundberg2017unified} to the trained classifiers. SHAP assigns each feature a contribution score for every individual prediction, grounded in cooperative game theory. For tree-based models, TreeSHAP computes exact Shapley values in polynomial time.

The explainability layer serves two purposes. At the global level, SHAP summary plots rank features by their overall contribution to the model's decisions, enabling researchers to validate that the model attends to structurally meaningful properties rather than spurious correlations. At the local level, individual SHAP waterfall plots allow security analysts to understand \textit{why} a specific QR code was flagged, which is essential for trust in deployed systems and for forensic analysis of novel attack variants.

\subsection{Statistical Characterization of Structural Differences}

Before training any classifier, we conducted a systematic statistical analysis of all 25 features to determine whether structural properties differ meaningfully between benign and malicious QR codes. Table~\ref{tab:statistical} presents the results for the five most discriminative features on the Trad et al.\ dataset, where the controlled QR version eliminates dimensional confounds.

Horizontal transition frequency emerges as the single most discriminative feature, with a Cohen's $d$ of $-0.76$ that approaches the conventional threshold for a large effect. Phishing QR codes exhibit \textit{fewer} horizontal transitions on average, which may seem counterintuitive given their greater payload complexity. However, longer URLs produce larger contiguous data blocks in the QR encoding, reducing the alternation between black and white modules along horizontal scan lines. Vertical transitions, by contrast, are \textit{higher} in phishing codes ($d = +0.68$), reflecting the way QR data codewords are arranged in vertical two-column strips that grow denser with payload length.

Density-based features (data region, bottom-right quadrant, overall module density) are all significantly elevated in phishing codes, consistent with the hypothesis that longer URLs require more black modules. All reported $p$-values are computed using Welch's $t$-test to account for unequal variance between classes.

\subsubsection{Spatial Hotspot Analysis}

To identify where in the QR grid the class differences concentrate, we computed the average binary matrix for each class and examined their per-position difference. Column~44 of the $69 \times 69$ grid showed the largest divergence ($p < 10^{-149}$), corresponding to the alignment pattern region in QR Version~13. The alignment pattern itself is class-invariant, but the data modules surrounding it are populated differently depending on payload length, creating a spatial signature that discriminates between classes.

This spatial analysis suggests that CNN-based models, which can learn position-dependent filters, may capture discriminative patterns that handcrafted features miss---a direction we explore in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

On the Trad dataset, all three classifiers achieve AUC values above 0.80 using our 25 engineered features. This falls below the 0.9133 reported by Trad and Chehab, which is expected: their approach uses all 4,761 raw pixel values as features, providing the classifier with direct access to every module position. Our 25 features compress this information into interpretable descriptors, trading some discriminative power for explainability and generalizability. The gap between raw-pixel and engineered-feature approaches quantifies the cost of interpretability, and we argue that this trade-off is worthwhile for deployed systems where analysts need to understand detection decisions.

On the CIC dataset, performance drops notably (AUC $\approx$ 0.65). This degradation has a clear cause: the CIC images are variable-resolution grayscale PNGs, not standardized binary matrices. Features like transition counts and run-length statistics are sensitive to image resolution and binarization thresholds, making them less reliable when QR codes span different versions and physical sizes. This result underscores a key limitation of handcrafted structural features and motivates a CNN-based alternative that can learn resolution-invariant representations directly from pixels.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalization}

We attempted cross-dataset transfer by training a Random Forest on CIC-extracted features and testing on the full Trad dataset. The classifier achieved an AUC of only 0.41, effectively predicting all samples as malicious. This failure is not surprising: the feature distributions differ substantially between grayscale PNG images and binary module matrices, even when computed using identical formulas. Transition counts in a 69$\times$69 binary matrix occupy an entirely different numerical range than those in a 200$\times$200 grayscale image, and standardization alone cannot bridge this gap.

This negative result is itself informative. It demonstrates that structural features, while statistically significant \textit{within} each dataset, do not automatically transfer across representation formats. Effective cross-dataset generalization requires either (a)~a normalization scheme that maps features to a format-invariant space, or (b)~a learned representation (e.g., from a CNN) that abstracts away low-level differences. We pursue the latter direction in our ongoing work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Attack Taxonomy}

Unsupervised clustering (K-Means, $k=3$) on the feature vectors of malicious QR codes from the CIC dataset reveals three distinct structural profiles:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Type A---High density.} Characterized by elevated module density and entropy, consistent with long obfuscated URLs containing tracking parameters and encoded payloads.
\item \textbf{Type B---Complex structure.} Distinguished by high transition frequencies in both axes, suggesting URLs with multiple redirect chains that produce intricate module arrangements.
\item \textbf{Type C---Subtle obfuscation.} Features that closely resemble benign QR codes, likely generated from short URL redirectors (e.g., bit.ly). These represent the hardest cases for structural detection alone and motivate the integration of contextual signals (e.g., accompanying message text) as a complementary modality.
\end{itemize}

This taxonomy is not merely descriptive; it has practical implications for detection system design. Type~A and Type~B attacks are reliably captured by structural features, while Type~C attacks represent a residual risk that structural analysis alone cannot fully mitigate.

At the individual prediction level, SHAP waterfall plots reveal interpretable decision pathways. For example, a QR code flagged as malicious with high confidence typically shows elevated vertical transitions, above-average module density, and low horizontal transitions---a pattern consistent with a long, complex URL encoded in the standard QR format. Conversely, false positives tend to be benign QR codes encoding legitimate but unusually long content (e.g., vCard contact information), which structural features alone cannot distinguish from phishing URLs.

\subsection{Discussion}

Our results support three claims. First, structural features are genuinely discriminative, not merely statistically significant: effect sizes in the medium-to-large range indicate practical, not just theoretical, separability between classes. Second, the discriminative patterns are grounded in the QR encoding specification itself---longer payloads produce denser, less horizontally alternating module patterns as a direct consequence of how data codewords are arranged in the QR matrix. This makes the approach robust to changes in phishing tactics, as the structural signature depends on payload \textit{length and complexity} rather than \textit{content}. Third, SHAP analysis confirms that the models rely on structurally meaningful features rather than spurious artifacts, lending confidence to deployment.

The primary limitation of our current approach is the difficulty in detecting Type~C attacks (short-URL redirects), where structural differences are minimal. We discuss strategies to address this limitation in the following section.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

We have presented an explainable, structural feature-based approach for detecting quishing attacks in QR codes that operates entirely without payload decoding. Our 25-feature framework captures geometric, density, complexity, spatial, and textural properties of QR images, and statistical analysis on two public datasets confirms that these features carry strong discriminative signal---particularly horizontal and vertical transition frequencies, which achieve medium-to-large effect sizes ($|d| > 0.68$).

The practical implications are noteworthy. Because our pipeline never decodes the QR payload, it can be deployed as a pre-screening layer that runs before any scanning application processes the QR code. The lightweight feature extraction and tree-based classification are compatible with mobile hardware, enabling on-device detection without cloud dependency---a relevant consideration for emerging markets with intermittent connectivity.

Several directions remain for future work. First, the Type~C attack category (short-URL redirects) motivates a multimodal extension that incorporates the textual content of accompanying messages. A semantic branch based on DistilBERT \cite{sanh2019distilbert}, fused with the visual features via late concatenation, could capture social engineering cues (urgency, authority, reward) that structural analysis misses. Second, replacing handcrafted features with a convolutional neural network such as MobileNetV2 \cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} would allow the model to learn spatial features directly from pixels, potentially improving performance on cases where our engineered features fall short. Our spatial analysis---which identified Column~44 as a discriminative hotspot---suggests that learned convolutional filters could capture position-dependent patterns more flexibly. Third, evaluation on adversarially perturbed QR codes \cite{wahid2026alfa} would clarify the robustness boundaries of structural detection. Finally, a user study integrating our detection system with a mobile scanning interface would assess real-world usability and acceptance.
