\begin{abstract}
QR code phishing---\textit{quishing}---has become a pressing cybersecurity concern as mobile payment adoption accelerates worldwide. The defensive pipeline against quishing has two natural intervention points: a \textit{pre-decode} stage where only the rendered QR image is available, and a \textit{post-decode} stage where the offline-decoded payload (typically a URL) is also available. Each stage carries a distinct signal, and detectors that operate at one stage in isolation discard the evidence of the other. We propose Q-Shield, a multimodal framework that combines (i) a Siamese contrastive visual branch built on a lightweight MobileNetV2 backbone, which classifies the QR image without any decoding, and (ii) a transformer-based text branch that consumes the offline-decoded URL string---decoded locally with no network call or payload execution---fused through a small late-fusion head with an explicit undecodability flag. On a combined benchmark of 21{,}998 QR samples drawn from the Trad et al.\ corpus and the CIC Trap4Phish 2025 dataset---an order of magnitude larger than the largest prior evaluation and spanning multiple QR versions and image resolutions---the fused configuration achieves AUC $0.975$, F1 $0.936$, recall $0.943$, and a default-threshold false-negative rate of $0.057$, already below the conventional $\leq 10\%$ security tolerance without retraining. The visual branch in isolation reaches AUC $0.915$ (two-seed ensemble with horizontal-flip test-time augmentation) and remains the operative signal whenever the QR payload cannot be decoded (low contrast, missing quiet zone, damaged finder pattern), so the multimodal model degrades gracefully to a calibrated visual-only detector in the worst case. Compared to the prior visual-only state of the art (Trad et al., AUC $0.913$ on a uniform single-version corpus), the fused model delivers a $+6.16$ pp AUC improvement on a benchmark eleven times larger and visually heterogeneous. Grad-CAM with per-dataset attention validation (spatial correlation $r=0.43$) explains the visual decisions, SHAP analysis on the 128-dimensional embedding describes the latent geometry, and the full multimodal model fits within mobile interaction budgets at $\sim$$100$\,ms CPU per scan.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%% ============================================================

Quick Response (QR) codes have become an essential component of modern digital interactions, enabling fast and convenient access to online services such as payments, authentication, and information sharing. Their adoption has increased significantly in recent years, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic, when contactless interactions became the norm. However, this rapid adoption has also introduced new security vulnerabilities, most notably QR code phishing attacks, commonly referred to as \textit{quishing}~\cite{sharevski2022phishing}.

At its core, quishing is a social-engineering attack rather than a purely technical one. Classic treatments of the discipline---Mitnick's account of human-factor exploitation~\cite{mitnick2002artdeception} and Hadnagy's systematic framework for social engineering~\cite{hadnagy2018socialengineering}---emphasize that the attacker's goal is to induce an impulsive, low-scrutiny action from the target. QR codes are almost perfectly suited to this purpose: they are opaque to the human eye, require a single gesture to activate, and are presented in contexts (restaurant menus, parking meters, invoice PDFs) that implicitly mark them as legitimate. Users thus develop a learned trust that attackers subsequently weaponize. The 2024 Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report identifies social-engineering-driven phishing as a leading initial access vector across industries~\cite{verizon2024dbir}, while the 2024 IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report places the average cost of phishing-originated breaches at several million dollars per incident~\cite{ibm2024cost}. Recent empirical studies further confirm that quishing succeeds as frequently as traditional phishing, yet is more difficult for users and conventional security systems to detect~\cite{weinz2025impact, geisler2024hooked}.

Existing approaches for detecting malicious QR codes split into two strands. URL-based detectors~\cite{wahsheh2021secure, khalifa2025multiphishnet} extract lexical and structural features from the decoded URL and classify it; the recent CIC Trap4Phish benchmark~\cite{nejati2026cic} reports that lightweight language models on decoded URLs reach $\geq$0.99 F1 on its corpus. The cost of this performance is the decoding requirement: a QR scanner must render the payload before classification, and QR codes can encode data beyond URLs (Wi-Fi credentials, payment information, contact records), so URL-based detection is necessarily incomplete~\cite{trad2025detecting}. Direct image-based detectors~\cite{wahid2025qris, trad2025detecting} avoid this requirement and apply when the payload is undecodable, but their reported performance ($\sim$0.91 AUC on uniform single-version corpora; CNN F1 $\sim$0.88 on heterogeneous CIC images~\cite{nejati2026cic}) lies measurably below the URL-side numbers. We argue that this gap is not an indictment of either strand but a consequence of treating them as alternatives rather than as layers of the same detector. Our framework therefore combines both: a Siamese visual branch that operates in the pre-decode regime, and an optional URL text branch that consumes the offline-decoded payload (decoding here is local, deterministic, and does not execute the URL---no fetch, no DNS, no JavaScript). A further challenge orthogonal to either branch is the lack of cross-dataset evaluation: prior reports use a single corpus, leaving open whether the numbers reflect generalization or dataset-specific artifacts~\cite{nejati2026cic, wahid2026alfa}. Q-Shield is trained and evaluated jointly across two independent corpora to address this gap.

\subsection{Research gaps}

From the literature surveyed above, we identify four concrete gaps that motivate the present work:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Single-stage detection.} Prior work treats pre-decode (image) and post-decode (URL) detection as competing alternatives, not as complementary layers of a single defender. The result is that visual-only methods are blind to URL-level signal even when offline decoding is feasible, while URL-only methods cannot help when decoding fails.
\item \textbf{Absence of cross-dataset evaluation.} Every prior study we are aware of reports results on a single dataset; none measures whether learned patterns transfer across independently collected QR corpora that differ in QR version, image format, or rendering pipeline.
\item \textbf{Limited dual-layer explainability.} Prior structural approaches provide either feature importance or handcrafted interpretability, but not both pixel-level (where does the model look?) and latent-level (which learned features drive the decision?) explanations.
\item \textbf{No operational deployment characterization.} Prior work reports a single operating point, typically at threshold $0.5$, without quantifying the recall/precision trade-off that an operator would need to meet a specific security tolerance (e.g., false-negative rate $\leq 10\%$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Our contribution}

In this paper, we propose \textbf{Q-Shield}, a multimodal framework that closes all four gaps simultaneously. Q-Shield is organized as two branches that share the same QR sample but operate at different stages of the detection pipeline. The visual branch, built on a Siamese MobileNetV2 backbone, classifies the QR image directly without any decoding, and is the only available signal when the payload cannot be decoded. The text branch consumes the offline-decoded URL string---decoded locally with no network call or payload execution---and refines the decision when decoding succeeds. A small late-fusion module combines both. The specific contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Multimodal pipeline aligned with the natural quishing defence stages.} The framework provides a pre-decode visual layer and a post-decode URL layer fused at the logit level. Each layer can act on its own; together they cover the cases where one of them has no signal to act on. This is, to our knowledge, the first quishing detector explicitly designed around the two-stage structure of the QR pipeline rather than choosing one stage over the other.

\item \textbf{Siamese contrastive visual branch.} The first application of Siamese networks with contrastive supervision to QR phishing detection. The backbone learns a metric space over QR images from paired supervision and yields an inter- to intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ in the learned embedding space.

\item \textbf{Cross-dataset generalization as a first-class design objective.} Q-Shield is trained and evaluated jointly on Trad~\cite{trad2025detecting} and CIC Trap4Phish 2025~\cite{nejati2026cic}, producing a 21{,}998-sample heterogeneous validation set spanning multiple QR versions and image formats---an order of magnitude larger than the largest prior evaluation.

\item \textbf{Operating-point guarantees for security deployment.} The fused multimodal model achieves FNR $0.057$ at the default threshold, already below the conventional $\leq 10\%$ security tolerance without any retraining or calibration. The visual fallback (used when the QR cannot be decoded) admits a calibrated threshold that reaches FNR $0.098$, so the system meets the target in both the decodable and the undecodable regimes.

\item \textbf{Dual-layer explainability, cross-validated.} We combine Grad-CAM visualization of convolutional attention with SHAP analysis of the learned embedding dimensions, and verify on each dataset separately (Pearson $r=0.43$ between class-difference attention maps) that the learned attention patterns are not dataset artifacts.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews prior work and situates Q-Shield relative to it. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the architecture, the loss functions used in each of the two training phases, and the datasets. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports main results, the ablation study, the cross-dataset benchmark, and the threshold-calibration analysis. Section~\ref{sec:xai} presents the explainability analysis. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses limitations and future directions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{URL- and Payload-Based Detection}

The earliest approaches to malicious QR code detection mirror email phishing pipelines: decode the URL, extract lexical and reputation features, classify. Wahsheh and Al-Zahrani~\cite{wahsheh2021secure} proposed a machine learning pipeline operating on URL string features extracted from scanned QR codes. Khalifa et al.~\cite{khalifa2025multiphishnet} introduced MultiPhishNet, a multimodal deep learning architecture combining URL text with visual representations. Nejati et al.~\cite{nejati2026cic} constructed the CIC Trap4Phish corpus, pairing QR images with URL metadata across multiple document formats, and evaluated classical ML classifiers on URL-derived features.

These methods demonstrate that, given a URL string, classification can achieve high accuracy. Their common limitation is the decoding requirement itself. A QR code must be scanned before URL-level analysis can begin, introducing both the exposure paradox and an incompatibility with non-URL payloads.

\subsection{Pixel-Level Visual Analysis}

Trad and Chehab~\cite{trad2025detecting} were, to our knowledge, the first to demonstrate that QR codes contain structural signals sufficient for phishing detection \textit{without decoding}. They trained XGBoost and related classifiers directly on the $4{,}761$ raw pixel values of $69\times69$ QR matrices, achieving AUC $0.913$ on a dataset of $9{,}987$ samples. Feature importance analysis revealed that specific pixel positions near alignment patterns carry disproportionate discriminative weight.

Wahid et al.~\cite{wahid2025qris} extended structural analysis to payment-specific QRIS codes with handcrafted features (module density, pattern regularity, error correction level). Their subsequent work~\cite{wahid2026alfa} evaluated robustness of these features under perturbation.

Our work builds on the \textit{no-decoding} insight of Trad and Chehab while departing from their methodology in three ways. First, we replace pixel-level classical ML with a deep CNN backbone (MobileNetV2), obtaining representations invariant to image resolution and QR version. Second, we train the backbone through Siamese contrastive learning rather than direct classification, which decouples feature learning from label memorization. Third, we validate on an order of magnitude more samples spanning multiple dataset origins.

\subsection{Commercial QR-Phishing Defenses}

Industry has produced several QR-phishing countermeasures that are worth contrasting with academic work. Commercial URL reputation services such as Google Safe Browsing and Microsoft Defender SmartScreen, as well as consumer-facing tools like Kaspersky QR Scanner and Norton Safe Web, flag known-malicious destinations after the QR code has been decoded and the URL has been looked up in a blocklist. These systems are effective against previously reported phishing campaigns but inherit two of the limitations enumerated above: they require decoding (introducing the exposure paradox), and they are ineffective against zero-day URLs or non-URL payloads. Q-Shield is therefore complementary rather than substitutive: it can act as a pre-scan filter that blocks suspicious QR images \textit{before} they are decoded, after which established blocklist services can handle the residual decoded URLs that pass the visual filter.

\subsection{Behavioral and Design-Centered Research}

A parallel line of work studies quishing from the user perspective. Sharevski et al.~\cite{sharevski2022phishing} demonstrated experimentally that users exhibit lower vigilance when scanning QR codes relative to clicking text links. Geisler et al.~\cite{geisler2024hooked} confirmed this finding in field conditions. Weinz et al.~\cite{weinz2025impact} documented the organizational impact of quishing on enterprise security. Bekavac and Garbin~\cite{bekavac2024qr} explored design interventions to make tampering visible at print time. These contributions establish why quishing succeeds but do not provide automated detection mechanisms. Our work is complementary: Q-Shield provides the technical detection layer; behavioral interventions address the human factor.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
%% ============================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} presents the full Q-Shield detection pipeline. The framework has two branches that share the same QR sample but operate at different stages.

\textbf{Visual branch.} An input QR image is first preprocessed to a standard 224$\times$224 grayscale tensor (steps 1--2), then passed through a MobileNetV2 backbone whose weights have been pretrained in Phase 1 via Siamese contrastive learning over image pairs (step 3). The backbone produces a 128-dimensional L2-normalized embedding (step 4), which feeds a classification head trained in Phase 2 with focal loss (step 5). At inference, a sigmoid maps the head's logit to a phishing probability (step 6); the Grad-CAM and SHAP branches (step 7) provide per-prediction explanations, and the operating threshold (step 8) is selected from the calibration table in Section~\ref{sec:calibration} according to the target security tolerance. The visual branch operates strictly on the rendered image: no decoding is performed, which makes it the only available layer when the payload cannot be recovered.

\textbf{Text branch.} When the QR can be decoded, we run a local pyzbar pass over the image and feed the resulting URL string to a transformer encoder (Section~\ref{sec:textbranch}). Decoding is local-only: it produces a string from the QR matrix and does not perform any network request, DNS resolution, or URL fetch, so the exposure paradox associated with browser-side scanning does not apply. Samples whose decode fails (low contrast, missing quiet zone, damaged finder pattern) are routed to an \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>} sentinel that the encoder learns as its own token, so undecodability itself becomes a feature.

\textbf{Fusion.} A small MLP combines the visual logit, the text logit, and a binary undecodability flag into the final phishing score (Section~\ref{sec:fusion}). Because both branches output a one-dimensional logit, the fusion head is tiny (3-input MLP, 3 hidden units) and trains in seconds on cached features.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Let $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ denote a grayscale QR code image. Our goal is to learn a classifier $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} \to [0, 1]$ that maps $x$ to the probability of being malicious, without decoding $x$ into its underlying payload. The function $f_\theta$ factors into two components:
\begin{equation}
f_\theta(x) = \sigma\big(g_\phi \circ h_\psi(x)\big),
\end{equation}
where $h_\psi: \mathbb{R}^{H \times W} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a convolutional embedding function producing a $d$-dimensional representation, $g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a classification head producing a logit, and $\sigma$ is the sigmoid nonlinearity. We train $h_\psi$ and $g_\phi$ in two phases.

\subsection{Siamese Backbone}

The backbone $h_\psi$ is MobileNetV2~\cite{sandler2018mobilenetv2} with three modifications. First, the input convolution is reduced from 3 channels to 1 channel to accept grayscale QR images; the pretrained weights are averaged across the RGB dimension to initialize the new layer. Second, the global average pooling output (1280 dimensions) is followed by a projection head with batch normalization, ReLU, dropout (rate 0.35), and a linear projection to $d = 128$. Third, the output is L2-normalized, placing all embeddings on the unit hypersphere. With these modifications, $h_\psi$ has 3.08M parameters.

The Siamese architecture shares $h_\psi$ between two image inputs $(x_1, x_2)$, producing embeddings $(e_1, e_2)$. We train the backbone with contrastive loss~\cite{chopra2005learning}, the canonical choice for Siamese metric learning as surveyed in recent reviews of deep learning objectives~\cite{elharrouss2024lossfunctions}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}(e_1, e_2, y) = (1-y)\frac{d^2}{2} + y\frac{\max(0, m - d)^2}{2},
\end{equation}
where $d = \|e_1 - e_2\|_2$, $y = 0$ for same-class pairs and $y = 1$ for different-class pairs, and $m = 1.5$ is the margin.

The loss has an intuitive interpretation. For same-class pairs ($y=0$), the term $d^2/2$ penalizes any residual distance, pulling embeddings of benign--benign or phishing--phishing pairs together. For different-class pairs ($y=1$), the term $\max(0, m-d)^2/2$ activates only if the distance is below the margin, pushing benign--phishing pairs apart until they satisfy $d \geq m$. The margin value $m = 1.5$ was selected by grid search over $\{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5\}$; lower margins induced premature convergence (underfitting at $m=1.0$) and higher margins slowed convergence without measurable gain. We chose contrastive over triplet loss for simplicity of batch construction (pairs are easier to sample uniformly than semi-hard triplets) and because contrastive supervision sufficed to produce an inter- to intra-class distance ratio of $1.94$ (Section~\ref{sec:xai}).

\subsection{Phase 1: Contrastive Pretraining}

Training pairs are sampled uniformly: each batch contains an equal split of same-class and different-class pairs, with class (benign or phishing) chosen uniformly at random. We train for up to 40 epochs with 60{,}000 pairs per epoch using AdamW (learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $2 \times 10^{-4}$) and cosine annealing with warm restarts every 15 epochs. Early stopping triggers after 8 epochs without improvement in validation contrastive loss. Horizontal flip augmentation is applied with probability 0.5; rotation is intentionally avoided because QR codes contain semantically meaningful orientation (finder patterns anchor the top-left, top-right, and bottom-left corners).

\subsection{Phase 2: Supervised Classification with Focal Loss}

The classification head $g_\phi$ stacks four linear layers with batch normalization, ReLU, and dropout: 128$\to$512$\to$128$\to$32$\to$1. We train with focal loss~\cite{lin2017focal}, which recent surveys~\cite{elharrouss2024lossfunctions} recommend over plain binary cross-entropy in settings where misclassification costs are asymmetric and hard examples dominate the error budget:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}(p, y) = -\alpha_y (1-p_y)^\gamma \log p_y,
\end{equation}
where $p_y$ is the predicted probability of the true class, $\alpha = 0.5$ (classes are approximately balanced after the combined-dataset sampling described below) and $\gamma = 2$. The factor $(1 - p_y)^\gamma$ down-weights easy examples (where $p_y$ is already high) and up-weights hard ones (where $p_y$ is small), focusing gradient signal on the phishing samples that the model currently misclassifies. In security-critical settings this is exactly the desired behavior: a missed phishing QR exposes the user to the attack, whereas a false positive merely triggers a benign warning. Our ablation confirms the practical effect: replacing focal loss with BCE raises false-negative rate from $0.202$ to $0.271$ with only a marginal change in AUC (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

To stabilize training, we freeze the backbone for the first five epochs and train only the head; after epoch 5, the entire network is unfrozen and fine-tuned end-to-end for fifteen additional epochs. Figures~\ref{fig:phase1} and~\ref{fig:phase2} visualize the training dynamics of both phases and justify the choice of best checkpoint.

\subsection{Text Branch: Offline-Decoded URL Classifier}
\label{sec:textbranch}

The text branch consumes the URL string recovered by an offline decoding pass over the QR image. The decoding stage is implemented with the pyzbar binding to libzbar, which reads the module pattern of the QR and returns the encoded payload as a UTF-8 string. This operation is local, deterministic, and does not perform any network call: it is the equivalent of reading text from a file on disk, and it cannot trigger the malicious behaviour associated with browser-side QR scanners. The classifier therefore inherits no exposure risk from this stage.

The encoder is a transformer model fine-tuned for binary phishing classification. We use DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} (66M parameters, $66\,$MB on disk) as the default backbone, with the [CLS] hidden state passed through dropout and a linear classifier of dimension $\text{hidden} \to 1$. Each input URL is tokenized with the model's pretrained tokenizer at maximum length 96, which covers the full URL distribution observed across both corpora. Samples whose decode fails are encoded as a single special token \texttt{<UNDECODABLE>}; the encoder thus learns a separate representation for the undecodable case, which is itself a phishing-relevant signal because adversaries occasionally generate QRs with damaged finder patterns to bypass scanners while remaining functional in some apps.

Training uses the same focal loss as the visual branch ($\gamma=2$, $\alpha=0.5$) for parity, AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ and weight decay $10^{-2}$, and a linear warmup-then-decay schedule with $10\%$ warmup. We train for three epochs on the train split (roughly 80k URLs); convergence is fast because URL classification is well within DistilBERT's pretraining domain. The trained encoder produces a one-dimensional logit per URL, which feeds the fusion head described next.

\subsection{Late Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

The fusion head consumes a three-dimensional vector $\mathbf{z} = [\ell_v, \ell_t, f] \in \mathbb{R}^3$, where $\ell_v$ is the visual branch logit, $\ell_t$ is the text branch logit, and $f \in \{0, 1\}$ is the undecodability flag. A two-layer MLP $g_{\text{fuse}}: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ with $16$ hidden units and ReLU activation maps $\mathbf{z}$ to the fused logit, and a sigmoid produces the final phishing probability. The fusion head has $81$ trainable parameters; both branch encoders are frozen during fusion training, so the entire fusion stage reduces to a tabular classification problem on cached features. We train with focal loss for $20$ epochs, AdamW (lr $10^{-3}$, wd $10^{-3}$), and report the checkpoint that maximises validation AUC.

We chose logit-level fusion over feature-level fusion (concatenating the 128-d visual embedding with the 768-d text pooled output) for two reasons. First, the logit-level form is interpretable: the learned coefficients quantify how much the fused decision relies on each branch and on the undecodability flag, an explanation that is directly actionable for security analysts. Second, the logit-level form decouples the fusion stage from the branch backbones at inference time. We report the feature-level variant as an ablation.

\subsection{Inference: TTA and Ensembling}
\label{sec:inference-method}

At inference we apply two refinements that improve accuracy without modifying the architecture or training procedure. First, \textit{horizontal-flip test-time augmentation (TTA)}: each input image $x$ is also evaluated as its horizontal flip $\tilde{x}$, and the final probability is the mean of the two passes, $\hat{p}(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\sigma(g_\phi h_\psi(x)) + \sigma(g_\phi h_\psi(\tilde{x}))\big)$. Although horizontal flipping is omitted from training augmentation only for vertical orientation, the model still benefits at test time from averaging two views of the same QR; the L/R asymmetry in the CIC corpus (Section~\ref{sec:xai}) makes this gain concrete. Second, a \textit{two-seed ensemble}: we train two Phase-1+Phase-2 pipelines that share data splits but differ in weight initialization and pair-sampling stochasticity (seeds $42$ and $7$). At inference, probabilities from the two models---each computed with TTA---are averaged. Because the data splits are identical, the diversity comes purely from optimization stochasticity, which is sufficient to decorrelate predictions and yield an additive gain over either model alone. The ensemble adds a $4\times$ inference cost (two models, two TTA passes each), characterized in Section~\ref{sec:inference}.

\subsection{Training Configuration}

The full hyperparameter set used to produce the Q-Shield checkpoint is the following. \textit{Backbone}: MobileNetV2 with ImageNet initialization, single-channel input at $224{\times}224$, projection head with dropout $0.35$, output embedding $128$-d L2-normalized. \textit{Classifier head}: $128{\to}512{\to}128{\to}32{\to}1$ with dropouts $0.4 / 0.3 / 0.2$. \textit{Phase 1}: contrastive loss with margin $m=1.5$ (selected by grid search), $60{,}000$ training pairs and $8{,}000$ validation pairs per epoch, AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $2\times10^{-4}$, cosine annealing with warm restarts of period $15$, up to $40$ epochs with patience $8$ on validation contrastive loss. \textit{Phase 2}: focal loss ($\gamma=2$, $\alpha=0.5$), $5$ epochs with frozen backbone (head learning rate $1\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $1\times10^{-4}$) followed by $15$ epochs of end-to-end fine-tuning (learning rate $1\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $2\times10^{-4}$), early stopping on validation AUC with patience $3$. \textit{Augmentation}: horizontal flip with $p=0.5$ in both phases; rotation and vertical flip are intentionally excluded because QR finder patterns encode orientation, so rotating a QR image changes its semantics and would inject label noise. All values are reproducible from the public notebooks.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Generalization}

To assess whether Q-Shield learns patterns that transfer across dataset origins or merely memorizes per-dataset artifacts, we conduct three cross-dataset experiments reported in the second section of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

We emphasize that these cross-dataset experiments are not a conventional generalization test; rather, they characterize the \textit{domain shift} between the two corpora. CIC images are variable-resolution grayscale PNGs subject to anti-aliasing and rendering artifacts, whereas Trad samples are clean $69 \times 69$ binary matrices. The two distributions differ at the pixel level in ways that exceed what mild augmentation can bridge.

When Q-Shield is trained exclusively on CIC and evaluated on Trad (CV1), the AUC is $0.72$ but the classifier effectively collapses toward the positive class (FNR $\approx 0$, implying precision degrades). The model retains discrimination ability, yet the threshold calibrated on CIC scores does not transfer to Trad's different score distribution. The reverse direction (CV2) is more severe: a backbone optimized on $69 \times 69$ binary matrices has not been exposed to the visual statistics of antialiased PNGs, and performance falls to near chance (AUC $0.52$).

These findings position combined training (CV3) as a form of \textit{domain-invariant learning}~\cite{ganin2015dann}: by including samples from both corpora during both phases of training, the backbone learns a representation robust to both sets of statistics simultaneously. The diversity of the combined corpus is therefore not a convenience but a methodological requirement. The result also has practical implications: metrics reported on a single, visually homogeneous dataset (e.g., a single QR version encoded with a single renderer) should be interpreted cautiously as upper bounds relative to real-world deployment conditions, where QR versions, capture resolutions, and rendering pipelines vary substantially.

Second, attention for both classes concentrates on the data-region modules that carry the encoded payload. This confirms that the model's decisions are driven by payload-dependent structure rather than by memorizing positional biases.

Notably, the finder patterns remain dark in both per-class averages, corroborating that the model does not rely on positional artifacts.

\subsubsection{Per-dataset validation of the attention pattern}

A natural concern is whether the left-vs-center-right asymmetry reported above reflects a genuine phishing-vs-benign signal or is an artifact of the visual statistics of the combined corpus (binary $69\times69$ Trad samples upsampled to $224\times224$ behave differently from natively antialiased CIC PNGs). To rule this out, we recomputed class-averaged Grad-CAM maps \textit{separately} on Trad and CIC validation samples and measured the pixel-wise correlation between the two difference maps (phishing $-$ benign). Figure~\ref{fig:gradcam_perdataset} shows both sets of maps side by side.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_gradcam_per_dataset.png}
\caption{Per-dataset Grad-CAM validation. Top row: class-averaged maps computed on Trad validation samples. Bottom row: same averages on CIC samples. \label{fig:gradcam_perdataset}

The pixel-wise correlation between the Trad and CIC difference maps is $r = 0.43$, a moderate positive value. In practical terms, this means that the model's attention pattern is neither purely universal (which would produce $r \approx 1$) nor purely dataset-specific (which would produce $r \approx 0$). Both datasets exhibit a right-side positive region consistent with phishing QRs encoding additional data codewords, but the exact spatial extent differs, reflecting the different module densities of each format. We read this result as evidence that Q-Shield's learned representation captures a genuine structural signal (positive correlation) while remaining sensitive to input format (correlation below unity). The combined-training approach is therefore both necessary (to expose the backbone to both formats) and effective (a shared signal does emerge).

\subsection{SHAP Analysis on Embedding Dimensions}

Treating the 128-dimensional Siamese embedding as a feature vector and the classifier head as the prediction function, we apply kernel SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017unified} to attribute each embedding dimension's contribution to individual predictions. Figure~\ref{fig:shap_dist} (left) ranks the top-20 dimensions by mean absolute SHAP value: the most important dimension contributes $|\text{SHAP}| \approx 0.016$, decreasing smoothly to around $0.005$ at position 20. The remaining 108 dimensions contribute negligibly. This suggests that the embedding is highly over-parameterized for binary classification, and a pruned 32- or 64-dimensional projection head would likely preserve performance while further reducing the memory footprint, which is valuable for mobile deployment.

First, the separation ratio greater than 1 confirms that contrastive training pulls same-class samples together and pushes different-class samples apart, as intended. Second, the phishing-phishing mean distance is \textit{smaller} than the benign-benign one, meaning that phishing QR codes are more tightly clustered in embedding space than benign ones. A plausible interpretation is that phishing URLs share structural regularities (common URL shorteners, redirect chains, query parameter patterns) that induce similar module distributions across different samples, whereas benign URLs span a more diverse distribution.

\subsection{Threshold Calibration for Security Deployment}
\label{sec:calibration}

The metrics in Table~\ref{tab:main} assume the conventional decision threshold of $p = 0.5$. For a security-critical detector, however, a calibrated threshold that privileges recall over precision is often preferable. Because AUC is threshold-independent and already high, the only design choice is where along the ROC curve to operate.

Table~\ref{tab:threshold} reports four operating points obtained by sweeping the decision threshold over $[0.05, 0.95]$ in increments of $0.025$ on a $\sim$22K-sample validation set. Sliding the threshold from $0.5$ toward lower values raises recall at the cost of precision, which in our application translates directly to reducing false negatives---the dominant source of harm in phishing detection. Crucially, an operating point of $p = 0.40$ reduces FNR from $0.182$ (default) to $0.098$, meeting the conventional $\leq 10\%$ security tolerance without retraining, while precision remains above $0.75$.

For completeness we also report probability-calibration diagnostics: Brier score $0.138$ and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) $0.129$. These values indicate that the raw sigmoid output is a good ranker but an imperfect probability estimator, a common property of networks trained with focal loss. Deployment scenarios that require genuine probability estimates (rather than binary decisions) would benefit from post-hoc temperature scaling; we do not explore this here because our target operational output is a calibrated binary decision.

The threshold sweep above is computed on the single-seed visual model to isolate the calibration analysis from the ensembling discussion. Two notes apply when reading it. First, the visual ensemble shifts the score distribution slightly (its precision at $p=0.5$ is higher and recall is comparable, see Table~\ref{tab:main}), but the qualitative trade-off curve and the existence of a calibrated operating point that meets FNR $\leq 0.10$ are preserved. Second, the multimodal fused configuration of Section~\ref{sec:multimodal-results} reaches FNR $0.057$ at the default threshold, so its threshold-calibration story is operationally a no-op: the security target is already met. We retain the calibration analysis because it characterizes the visual fallback regime---when the QR cannot be decoded, the system reduces to the visual branch and the calibrated threshold becomes the relevant operating point.

\section{Discussion, Limitations, and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{What Q-Shield Establishes}

Q-Shield demonstrates that combining a pre-decode visual branch with a post-decode URL text branch through a small late-fusion head produces a detector that surpasses both visual-only and prior multimodal proposals on a heterogeneous benchmark, while remaining small enough for mobile deployment. Four claims are supported by the evidence:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Multimodal fusion is the operationally dominant configuration.} The fused model reaches AUC $0.9749$, F1 $0.936$, and FNR $0.057$ on the joint $21{,}998$-sample benchmark, exceeding Trad et al.'s visual-only $0.913$ AUC by $+6.16$ pp and the visual-only ensemble of Q-Shield by $+6.03$ pp. The default-threshold FNR is already below the conventional $\leq 10\%$ security tolerance, so threshold calibration becomes optional rather than load-bearing.
\item \textit{The visual branch is a graceful fallback, not redundant.} Removing the visual branch and operating only on the decoded URLs costs $-1.57$ pp AUC and $-0.012$ F1 (Table~\ref{tab:multimodal}). The gap is small in aggregate but concentrated on the QRs whose payload cannot be decoded, where the visual branch is the only signal available; the fusion's undecodability flag lets the head learn this fallback automatically.
\item \textit{Learned representations generalize better than handcrafted ones.} Our handcrafted feature baselines plateau near $0.81$; the single-seed Siamese visual model adds $0.09$ AUC and the multimodal fusion adds $0.16$, with no additional feature engineering.
\item \textit{The architecture is interpretable at both levels.} Grad-CAM and SHAP together provide pixel-level and feature-level explanations of the visual branch, closing a common gap in prior structural-feature work; per-dataset Grad-CAM correlation ($r = 0.43$) confirms the visual signal is structural rather than dataset-specific.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

We acknowledge the following limitations explicitly.

\textbf{Visual-only false negative rate at default threshold.} The visual branch in isolation has FNR $0.199$ (full ensemble) or $0.202$ (single seed) at threshold $0.5$, above the $\leq 0.10$ security target. The fused multimodal configuration brings this down to $0.057$ at the same threshold, so the limitation only applies when the URL is undecodable and the system falls back to the visual branch. For deployments where every sample must clear the $\leq 0.10$ target regardless of decode success, the visual fallback can use the calibrated threshold from Section~\ref{sec:calibration} to reach FNR $0.098$ at a modest precision cost. Cost-sensitive fine-tuning of the visual branch is a natural direction for closing this fallback gap further.

\textbf{Phase-2 overfitting.} During supervised fine-tuning we observed gradual validation-loss divergence after the eighth epoch---training loss continued to decrease while validation loss increased from $0.053$ to $0.103$ by epoch 20. Early stopping (patience $=3$ on val AUC) selects the checkpoint that maximizes generalization, but the phenomenon indicates that our backbone capacity slightly exceeds the information content of the current training corpus. Stronger regularization (MixUp, label smoothing) and additional training data would likely mitigate this further.

\textbf{Unverified URL overlap between datasets.} The Trad dataset does not redistribute URL strings with its pickled QR matrices, preventing direct URL-level overlap checks with CIC. Both datasets draw from different PhishTank crawls and benign corpora (Alexa vs.\ Majestic) recorded at different time windows, which makes substantial overlap unlikely, but this has not been verified empirically. A future release of our pipeline will re-run the full evaluation with a documented overlap check.

\textbf{Resolution-dependent performance.} As reported in Section~\ref{sec:resolution}, Q-Shield's accuracy degrades for QR images above $246$\,px per side (AUC $0.844$, FNR $0.311$), compared to AUC $0.940$ on smaller images. The root cause is information loss during the fixed $224 \times 224$ input resize. Multi-scale training (exposing the backbone to larger input sizes during training) or a resolution-adaptive backbone would likely close this gap.

\textbf{Probability calibration.} The raw sigmoid outputs of Q-Shield have Brier score $0.138$ and Expected Calibration Error $0.129$, indicating that the model is a good ranker but an imperfect probability estimator. Applications requiring genuine probability estimates (e.g., risk scoring) should apply post-hoc temperature scaling; binary-decision deployments use the calibrated threshold from Section~\ref{sec:calibration} and are not affected.

\textbf{Out-of-QR context.} Q-Shield considers the QR sample (image plus its decoded payload) in isolation, whereas a fielded QR code arrives embedded in a larger context (email subject, SMS body, sender metadata). Extending the framework with a third branch over this surrounding text would likely yield a further reduction in false negatives, but validating it at scale requires paired QR+message corpora that are not publicly available; we leave this as future work.

\textbf{Container-format stripping.} Both source corpora are curated differently---Trad ships binary $69\times69$ matrices and CIC Trap4Phish ships QR images already extracted from multi-format email attachments (PDF, SVG, DOCX, HTML). Our pipeline unifies these inputs by rendering every sample as a grayscale PNG of $224\times224$ pixels before the CNN, which discards the \textit{container} in which the QR was delivered. In practice the container itself carries phishing-relevant signal (PDF macros, SVG-embedded scripts, sender headers) that a visual-only classifier cannot observe. Q-Shield should therefore be regarded as one layer of a multi-stage detector rather than a complete email-security system.

\textbf{Adversarial robustness.} We have not evaluated Q-Shield against adversarial perturbations of QR modules. Targeted attacks (e.g., patching a small region to flip the prediction) remain an open research question.

\subsection{Future Work}

We identify the following concrete extensions, each worthy of dedicated study:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Surrounding-context branch.} The current multimodal pipeline fuses the QR image with its offline-decoded URL. A natural extension is a third branch over the message context that surrounds the QR (email subject, SMS body, sender metadata), fused with the existing two branches. We expect this to be the most direct path to reducing the false negative rate below 10\% in production email gateways.

\item \textbf{Localized deployment.} Q-Shield is country-agnostic by construction, but region-specific phishing campaigns exploit local institutions and linguistic cues. A localized dataset collection and lightweight fine-tuning strategy would enable per-region deployment (e.g., Peruvian financial-services ecosystem, Indian UPI, Brazilian Pix).

\item \textbf{Multi-scale training.} The resolution-stratified analysis (Table~\ref{tab:persize}) shows performance degradation on large QR images. Training with multiple input resolutions---or adopting a backbone that natively supports variable input sizes---would close this gap without increasing inference cost at deployment.

\item \textbf{Adversarial robustness.} Empirical evaluation of Q-Shield under module-level adversarial perturbations, with defenses based on adversarial training and randomized smoothing.

\item \textbf{Provenance-aware detection.} Q-Shield answers the question \textit{``is this QR visually indistinguishable from a phishing QR?''} but not \textit{``was this QR generated by the authorized source?''}---two independent components of real-world QR security. Mobile payment ecosystems in Latin America illustrate the distinction concretely. Peru's dominant mobile payment platform, Yape (operated by BCP), generates merchant QRs through an authenticated backend; the visual artifact shown at point-of-sale is expected to originate from that authenticated path. An attacker who physically overlays a malicious QR on a legitimate merchant's display, or who injects a rogue QR at the UI layer of a compromised terminal, can produce an attack QR whose \textit{contents} are perfectly well-formed and whose \textit{visual statistics} match benign distributions, evading a content-based detector entirely. Addressing this gap requires complementary provenance signals: cryptographic signatures embedded in the QR payload, merchant-ID validation against the issuer, session-bound tokens, and geolocation or capture-device attestation. A next-generation anti-quishing stack would combine a visual classifier like Q-Shield (for zero-day content-based attacks) with a provenance verifier (for content-valid but origin-invalid attacks). We view these as orthogonal and complementary layers rather than competitors.
\end{enumerate}
